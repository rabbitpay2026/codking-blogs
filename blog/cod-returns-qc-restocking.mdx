      <div className="ck-summary"><div className="ck-summary-label">Summary</div><p>By the time a refused COD parcel reaches your dock, every rupee you were going to lose on freight, handling and the unearned sale is already gone. The only money still in play is the unit inside the box, and that value decays with every day it sits ungraded. This is a guide to the reverse bench: reconciling what the courier says it returned against what actually arrived, matching each parcel to its order, grading on a written four-step scale at the moment of opening, attaching a disposition rule to each grade in advance, and keeping graded stock out of your main sellable pool until it is genuinely as-new. It also covers when a repack pays for itself, which categories can never be restocked whatever their condition, how to keep Shopify from quietly overselling recovered units, and the four counts that tell you how much reverse value you are actually recovering.</p></div>

      <div className="ck-body">
        <p className="ck-lede">Most of what is written about COD returns is about preventing them, and rightly so — the cheapest return is the one that never ships. But some share of your parcels will always come back, and when they do, the decision you make in the first few minutes of handling them is worth more than anything else you can still do with that order. The forward loss is sunk. The unit is not. Yet in most warehouses the returned parcels are the lowest-priority pile in the building, opened when there is time, and the gap between a brand that recovers most of its reverse inventory and one that recovers little is almost entirely a bench process.</p>

        <h2 id="different">Why a COD return is a harder QC problem than a customer return</h2>

        <p>If you also take prepaid orders, you already handle customer-initiated returns, and it is tempting to run RTO parcels through the same process. They are not the same problem, and the differences all make the RTO parcel harder.</p>

        <p><strong>There is no return request.</strong> A customer return arrives with an RMA, a stated reason, and a customer you can contact. An RTO parcel arrives with a courier reason code entered by someone in a hurry, and nobody to ask. You cannot call the buyer and establish what happened, because from their side nothing happened.</p>

        <p><strong>It is older than you think.</strong> A customer return is dispatched deliberately, usually promptly. An RTO parcel may sit at a delivery branch through two or three reattempts, then travel the reverse network on a lower priority than forward freight. The unit inside has been in the system far longer than the forward leg alone suggests, which matters enormously for anything seasonal, dated or fashion-led.</p>

        <p><strong>It arrives in batches, unannounced.</strong> Reverse consolidation means parcels turn up in bulk rather than in a steady trickle, often the week after a festive or campaign peak, which is exactly when your team has the least slack. The pile is largest when nobody has time for it.</p>

        <p><strong>It may not be identifiable.</strong> A forward label that has abraded, been torn across the opening seam, or been overlaid with a reverse label can arrive unreadable. A customer return at least has a form inside. An unidentifiable RTO parcel is inventory you cannot attribute to an order, cannot claim against, and cannot analyse.</p>

        <p><strong>You do not know what happened to it.</strong> It may have been refused at the door untouched, or opened at the door and refused after inspection, or opened at a branch. The parcel's own condition is the only evidence, which is why the grading and the photographs described below carry so much weight.</p>

        <h2 id="aging">The real cost is aging, not damage</h2>

        <p>Operators usually assume the loss on a returned unit is physical: something broke. In practice, for most catalogues, the larger loss is time. A unit that comes back perfectly intact and then sits in a corner for six weeks has lost value in several ways at once.</p>

        <p>It was not available to sell during a period when you may have been out of stock on that very SKU, so you lost the margin twice — once on the refused order and once on a sale you could have made from recovered stock. It has aged against its season, its collection or its expiry window. It has accumulated handling risk, because loose parcels in a corner get moved, stacked and opened by people looking for something else. And it has become harder to grade honestly: after six weeks, nobody can tell whether the scuff happened in transit or on your floor, which removes any chance of a courier claim and makes your own SKU analysis unreliable.</p>

        <p>So the single most useful target in reverse logistics is not a damage rate. It is the number of days between a parcel arriving at your dock and the unit inside it being either back in sellable stock or formally written off. Measure that interval, and most of the other improvements in this article follow from trying to shorten it.</p>

        <h2 id="reconcile">Start upstream: reconcile what was returned against what arrived</h2>

        <p>Before any grading process is worth building, you need to know whether the parcels are reaching you at all. Four counts, in order, for a defined period:</p>

        <p><strong>One.</strong> Orders dispatched that were not delivered — your own record from the order table.</p>

        <p><strong>Two.</strong> Returns the courier reports as initiated — from the courier panel or the RTO manifest.</p>

        <p><strong>Three.</strong> Parcels physically received at your dock and logged.</p>

        <p><strong>Four.</strong> Units graded and dispositioned.</p>

        <p>Every brand has leakage between these numbers and almost none of them know the size of it. The gap between one and two is undelivered parcels the courier has not yet started returning, some of which are sitting in a branch and can still be re-attempted. The gap between two and three is parcels that were supposed to come back and did not — lost on the reverse leg, and in most courier contracts eligible for a claim if you raise it inside the window. The gap between three and four is your own backlog, and it is the one nobody counts because the parcels are physically present and therefore feel accounted for.</p>

        <p>Reconcile this monthly against the courier's own return manifest, parcel by parcel for the exceptions. The claim window on a reverse-leg loss is typically short and it starts without anyone telling you, so a quarterly reconciliation finds problems after they have expired. The general discipline of reading courier records against your own is the same one that recovers money on <a href="/blog/courier-weight-disputes">weight disputes</a>, and it tends to be the same person who is good at both.</p>

        <h2 id="identify">Identify before you open</h2>

        <p>The first action at the bench is matching the parcel to an order, and it has to happen before the tape is cut, because the moment the parcel is open you have lost the ability to document its arrival condition.</p>

        <p>In sequence: photograph the sealed parcel as received, with whatever label is readable in frame. Scan or key the AWB and resolve it to the order. Log the receipt against that order with the date. Only then open it.</p>

        <p>Two things make this fast rather than painful. First, label placement on the forward leg — a label on the largest flat face, clear of the opening seam and the corners, survives the round trip and stays scannable. Second, a legible brand name and return address printed on the parcel itself, so that a parcel whose label is destroyed is at least identifiable as yours and can be traced by its contents.</p>

        <p>For parcels that genuinely cannot be matched, keep a dedicated unidentified bin and work it once a week rather than letting those parcels obstruct the main flow. Most can be resolved by opening them and matching the contents against the small set of orders in your unreconciled list. The ones that cannot are a shrink figure worth knowing: it is a direct measure of your labelling discipline, and it is usually larger than anyone in the business would guess.</p>

        <h2 id="grade">The grading scale, and why it must be written down</h2>

        <p>Grading is where the value is recovered or lost, and the failure mode is always the same: the grade becomes a judgement call made differently by different people on different days, which makes the resulting data useless and the disposition inconsistent.</p>

        <p>The fix is a written scale with physical tests, not adjectives. Four grades is the right number — three is too coarse to separate "repack and sell as new" from "sell as second quality", and five invites debate at the bench.</p>

        <p><strong>Grade A — sellable as new.</strong> The outer is clean enough to ship, the inner seal or polybag is unbroken, all components and accessories are present, tags are attached, and nothing has been used or tried on. This unit goes back into main stock, ideally the same day.</p>

        <p><strong>Grade B — sellable as new after a repack.</strong> The product itself meets the Grade A test, but the presentation does not: the outer carton is scuffed or crushed, the inner packaging is torn, a tag has come off, a box insert is bent. A unit here becomes Grade A for the cost of new packaging and a few minutes of labour.</p>

        <p><strong>Grade C — sellable, but not as new.</strong> The product has been opened or handled, has a cosmetic mark, is missing a non-essential accessory, or the primary packaging is part of the product and cannot be replaced. It is functionally fine and somebody will buy it at a discount, but you cannot send it to a full-price customer.</p>

        <p><strong>Grade D — not sellable.</strong> Functionally damaged, contaminated, missing an essential component, or in a category that cannot be resold at all regardless of condition. This unit is a write-off and the only remaining decision is whether a courier or insurance claim applies.</p>

        <p>Write the tests for each grade on a laminated card at the bench, in the language your team actually uses, with the category-specific cases spelled out — for apparel, whether a garment has been worn; for cosmetics, whether a seal is broken; for electronics, whether the activation or warranty seal is intact. The person grading should never have to interpret.</p>

        <p>Record the grade against the order, not just against the SKU. Grade tied to the order is what lets you cut the data by courier, lane, SKU and NDR reason afterwards. Grade tied only to the SKU tells you almost nothing.</p>

        <p>One distinction worth keeping clear: this grade answers "what can I do with this unit now", while the four-bucket audit in <a href="/blog/packaging-damage-cod-refusals">packaging and COD refusals</a> answers "why was this parcel refused". They are recorded at the same bench from the same parcel and they feed different decisions. Capture both in the same pass; it costs one extra column.</p>

        <h2 id="disposition">Decide the disposition before the parcel arrives</h2>

        <p>The most expensive habit in reverse logistics is deciding what to do with each returned unit when you look at it. It is slow, it is inconsistent, and it produces the pile.</p>

        <p>Instead, write a disposition rule for each grade, per product class, in advance. The bench then has no decision to make: it reads the grade, applies the rule, and the unit moves. A rule set looks like this.</p>

        <p><strong>Grade A → main sellable stock, same day.</strong> No approval, no holding area. The whole point of a grade is that it is already the approval.</p>

        <p><strong>Grade B → repack queue, with a cut-off.</strong> Repack if the queue can be cleared within a stated number of days; otherwise downgrade to C. An unbounded repack queue is just the pile with a better name.</p>

        <p><strong>Grade C → second-quality channel at a pre-set price.</strong> The price should already be decided per product class before any unit arrives, because pricing case by case is how Grade C stock ends up never being listed at all.</p>

        <p><strong>Grade D → write off this week, claim where applicable.</strong> Scrap, recycle or dispose per category. Do not keep Grade D stock in the hope of a use for it.</p>

        <p>The rule set is also where you encode the categories that can never be restocked. Anything with a broken hygiene seal, anything consumable, anything with a shelf life close to its limit, anything where resale as new would be a compliance or safety question — these are Grade D by policy regardless of how good the unit looks. Decide this once, write it down, and remove it from the bench's discretion entirely. The temptation to restock a returned sealed product that looks untouched is strongest exactly where the consequences are worst.</p>

        <h2 id="repack">When a repack pays, and when it is a hobby</h2>

        <p>Grade B is the grade with real money in it, and also the one where effort most often exceeds recovery. The test is small enough to do once per product class and then stop thinking about.</p>

        <p>On the cost side: the replacement packaging, the labour time at your actual loaded hourly cost, any replacement tag or insert, and the holding cost of the days the unit waits in the queue. On the recovery side: the difference between the full price you can now charge and the second-quality price you would otherwise have taken, multiplied by your realistic confidence that the unit sells at all in a reasonable window.</p>

        <p>Run that per product class and the answer is usually stark. High-margin items with cheap outer packaging are worth repacking almost always. Low-margin items with complex or branded primary packaging — printed boxes, fitted inserts, shrink wrap you cannot reapply — are usually not, and should be routed straight to Grade C treatment. The cases in the middle are rare, and the right call for them is whichever keeps the queue moving.</p>

        <p>Two practical notes. Keep flat-packed replacement outers and spare tags at the bench in the quantities you actually consume, because a repack queue blocked on a packaging order is the most avoidable backlog there is. And batch the repacking into a scheduled block rather than doing it parcel by parcel, since the setup time dominates.</p>

        <h2 id="channel">Where Grade C stock actually goes</h2>

        <p>Second-quality stock only becomes money if there is a standing channel for it. Building the channel before you need it is the difference between a clearing route and a corner of the warehouse.</p>

        <p>The options available to most Indian D2C brands, roughly in order of how much margin they preserve:</p>

        <p><strong>A declared outlet or seconds section on your own store.</strong> Highest recovery, because you keep the full retail relationship, and it is honest if the listing states plainly what the condition is. Needs separate inventory handling so a seconds unit can never be shipped against a full-price order.</p>

        <p><strong>Bundling.</strong> A Grade C unit included as an add-on, a sample, or part of a value pack, with the condition stated. Works best for consumables and accessories.</p>

        <p><strong>Marketplace seconds or open-box listings.</strong> Where the platform supports a used or open-box condition properly. Lower recovery, but genuine demand and no effort spent on traffic.</p>

        <p><strong>Local or staff sales.</strong> Fast, cash-positive, no listing effort, and appropriate for small volumes. Keep it on a documented price list so it does not become a favour.</p>

        <p><strong>Bulk liquidation.</strong> The lowest recovery per unit and the right answer when the volume exceeds what the channels above can absorb, or when the stock is aging against a season. The mistake is reaching for it first; the other mistake is refusing to reach for it at all while stock dies on the shelf.</p>

        <p>Whichever mix you run, two rules hold. Describe the condition accurately in the listing — a seconds sale that generates a complaint has cost you more than the write-off would have. And set the price before the stock arrives, so that the decision at the bench is purely which channel, never how much.</p>

        <h2 id="inventory">Keeping recovered stock from quietly overselling</h2>

        <p>A returned unit restocked carelessly causes a second failure weeks later, and it is usually an inventory-records problem rather than a physical one.</p>

        <p>In Shopify terms, the mechanics that matter: a cancelled or refunded order does not necessarily restock the item unless you choose to, so an RTO that was never cancelled properly leaves your available quantity understated while the physical unit sits in the building. Conversely, a returned unit added back to the same variant at the same location becomes indistinguishable from new stock, which is exactly what you do not want for Grade C.</p>

        <p>The clean pattern is to separate them structurally. Grade A and repacked Grade B go back to the main location and the main variant, because they are genuinely as-new. Grade C goes to a distinct location, a distinct variant, or a separate seconds product — never into the pool that full-price orders draw from. Grade D leaves inventory entirely through a recorded adjustment with a reason, not by disappearing.</p>

        <p>Then make the restock a step in the bench process with a name, rather than something that happens eventually. The test of whether this is working: pick any returned order from a month ago and trace the unit. If you cannot say which bin it is in or which adjustment removed it, your reverse inventory is not under control regardless of what the dashboard says.</p>

        <h2 id="bench">The bench itself</h2>

        <p>This is a physical process and the physical setup determines whether it gets done.</p>

        <p>Give returns a dedicated station, not a shared packing table — a surface, a scanner, a phone or camera on a stand, a bin per grade, the laminated grading card, the repack materials, and a bin for unidentified parcels. If returns have to be cleared off a table before anything else can happen there, they will be cleared onto the floor.</p>

        <p>Work to a one-touch rule: a parcel is picked up once and leaves the bench in a grade bin. No "set aside to look at later", because later is where the pile comes from. If a parcel genuinely cannot be decided — a possible claim, an unidentifiable unit, a grading question nobody can answer — it goes in the exceptions bin, and the exceptions bin is worked on a fixed day each week.</p>

        <p>Staff it against the inbound curve, not the average. Reverse volume arrives in waves that lag your order peaks by weeks, which is the same lag that catches people out in a <a href="/blog/festive-season-cod-operations">festive peak</a>: the orders are long over, the returns are still landing, and the temporary staff have gone. Plan reverse-bench hours for the weeks after a peak specifically, and give the role to someone consistent rather than rotating it, because grading quality depends on the same person applying the same tests.</p>

        <h2 id="feedback">What the grade mix tells you upstream</h2>

        <p>Once a few hundred parcels have been graded against orders, the data answers questions that nothing else in your stack can.</p>

        <p><strong>Grade mix by courier and lane.</strong> If one courier's returns arrive substantially more degraded than another's on comparable lanes, that is a reverse-handling difference, and it belongs in your <a href="/blog/courier-allocation-cod-orders">allocation decisions</a> alongside delivery success. Most brands allocate on forward performance only and never price the condition of what comes back.</p>

        <p><strong>Grade mix by SKU.</strong> SKUs that return as Grade C or D while the rest of the catalogue returns as A are telling you about packaging, fragility, or a product whose primary packaging cannot survive a round trip. That is a packaging spec decision, and it is narrow and cheap when it is driven by this data rather than applied across the catalogue.</p>

        <p><strong>Grade mix by NDR reason.</strong> Parcels returned as "refused" that arrive opened are a different event from parcels refused unopened, and the difference changes what verification can do for you.</p>

        <p><strong>Recovery rate overall.</strong> The share of returned units that end up back in sellable stock, by value. This is the number that tells you whether the bench process is worth the hours, and it is the one to put in front of whoever approves the staffing. It also feeds directly into the salvage line of your per-return cost figure — the one built in <a href="/blog/cod-return-cost-reverse-logistics">what a COD return actually costs you</a> — so improving recovery lowers the cost you load onto every delivered order.</p>

        <h2 id="order">What to change first</h2>

        <p>In the order that returns the most for the least effort:</p>

        <p><strong>Log and photograph every returned parcel on arrival, before opening.</strong> Costs minutes, makes claims possible, and creates the only reliable record of arrival condition.</p>

        <p><strong>Write the four-grade card and put it at the bench.</strong> One afternoon of work. It converts a judgement call into a lookup.</p>

        <p><strong>Write the disposition rules, including the never-restock categories.</strong> Another afternoon. This is what stops the pile forming.</p>

        <p><strong>Separate Grade C from main inventory.</strong> A distinct location or variant, so recovered stock can never ship against a full-price order.</p>

        <p><strong>Reconcile the four counts monthly.</strong> Dispatched-not-delivered, returns initiated, parcels received, units dispositioned. The gaps are where money is leaking.</p>

        <p><strong>Then run the repack economics per product class</strong> and set the queue cut-off. This is the optimisation step, and it only pays once the four above are in place.</p>

        <h2 id="limits">Where verification fits, and where it does not</h2>

        <p>Nothing in this article reduces your return rate. It changes how much of each returned parcel you keep, which is a different and smaller lever than not shipping the parcel at all — and that is the right order of priority: prevent first, recover second.</p>

        <p>COD King works on the prevention side. It confirms order intent before dispatch, applies COD rules by pincode, cart value and order history, and collects a partial advance where the risk justifies it, so that fewer parcels enter the reverse leg in the first place. It has nothing to say about what condition those parcels arrive back in, or what you should do with them. That part is a bench, a laminated card, and somebody who opens every box the week it lands.</p>

        <h2 id="short">The short version</h2>

        <p>When a refused COD parcel reaches your dock, the freight, the handling and the unearned margin are already lost; the unit inside is the only recoverable value, and it decays with every day it goes ungraded. Reconcile what the courier says it returned against what actually arrived, because the parcels that never come back are claimable and nobody checks. Identify and photograph each parcel before cutting the tape. Grade it at the bench on a written four-step scale with physical tests, not adjectives, and record the grade against the order so you can cut the data by SKU, lane and courier later. Attach a disposition rule to each grade in advance — same-day restock, bounded repack queue, pre-priced seconds channel, same-week write-off — so the bench never has a decision to make. Keep second-quality stock structurally out of the pool that full-price orders draw from, and make never-restock categories a policy rather than a judgement. Then measure the one interval that governs everything else: days from arrival to either sellable or written off.</p>
