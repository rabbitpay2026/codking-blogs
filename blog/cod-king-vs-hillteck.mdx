      <div className="ck-summary"><div className="ck-summary-label">Summary</div><p>Both apps verify cash on delivery orders over IVR and WhatsApp, send OTPs, recover abandoned carts and push buyers towards prepaid, and if that is your whole requirement either will do it. They diverge on three things: whether the order is captured through a replacement COD form, what you are able to do to an order that looks risky, and how much marketing tooling comes attached.       <div className="ck-body">
        <p className="ck-lede">Search the Shopify App Store for a way to stop fake COD orders and both of these apps appear on the first screen with nearly interchangeable copy: verify COD orders, IVR, WhatsApp, reduce RTO, convert to prepaid. The overlap is real. It is also narrower than the copy suggests, and the parts that do not overlap are the parts that decide which one fits the operation you actually run.</p>

        <p>A note on method, because it changes how you should read this. Everything factual below comes from each app's own Shopify App Store listing and, for HillTeck, its own website, read on 21 November 2026. Where a listing does not mention a capability, this article says so in exactly those words rather than claiming the app lacks it — app listings are marketing pages, not specifications, and plenty of shipped features never make it onto them. Plans, prices and ratings on both listings change; verify before you decide. And since one of these apps is ours, the fair thing to do is to tell you where it is the wrong choice, which is a section further down.</p>

        <p>One small housekeeping point: the brand writes itself HillTeck on its own site, while its Shopify listing heading currently reads "Hillteck: Verify COD Orders". Same company. It also publishes more than one listing in this category; this compares the one at the <code>cod-order-verification</code> slug.</p>

        <h2 id="what-each-is">What each one is built around</h2>

        <p>The quickest way to tell two similar apps apart is to read the first sentence of each description, because that is where a vendor says what it thinks it is.</p>

        <p>HillTeck's listing opens by describing itself as purpose-built to detect and reduce RTO losses and as a WhatsApp marketing automation platform. It describes sending automated IVR calls and WhatsApp messages to verify both COD and prepaid orders, and adding a label to the Shopify order when an order is confirmed or cancelled so the list can be filtered. Its own site extends well past verification: campaigns and broadcasts, a multi-team inbox, a chatbot builder, OTP login, Instagram DM automation, WhatsApp CRM and commerce, click-to-WhatsApp ads. The centre of gravity is the messaging channel, with COD verification as one of the things you do through it.</p>

        <p>COD King's listing opens on the payment decision: stop fake cash on delivery orders, verify buyers by OTP, take partial COD, switch COD to prepaid. The features it lists follow from that — a one-step COD form on product pages and cart, OTP over SMS or WhatsApp, a small partial payment on risky orders with rules by pincode, cart value, product or RTO risk, confirmation over WhatsApp, SMS or IVR with auto-cancellation of unconfirmed orders, and COD fees or prepaid discounts to move buyers to prepaid. The centre of gravity is what the order is allowed to be.</p>

        <p>That difference in orientation is not a feature gap, and it is worth holding onto because it explains most of what follows. The same verification call means different things depending on what the system is allowed to do with the answer.</p>

        <h2 id="overlap">Where they genuinely overlap</h2>

        <p>A large part of the middle is common ground, and anyone telling you otherwise is selling.</p>

        <p>Both listings describe OTP verification. Both describe verification over WhatsApp. Both describe automated voice calls — HillTeck's bullets lead with automated voice call verification for COD orders and its site leads with IVR, while COD King's description lists IVR as one of three confirmation channels. Both describe abandoned cart recovery over WhatsApp. Both describe order confirmation and shipment tracking messages. Both describe moving COD buyers to prepaid with incentives. Both carry an address validation feature tag, neither explains what it does, and you should ask both what that actually means in practice, because "address validation" covers everything from a pincode format check to a genuine deliverability lookup.</p>

        <p>So if your requirement is "call and message the customer before dispatch, and cancel the order if nobody confirms", both apps describe doing that, and a feature-list comparison will not separate them. What separates them at that point is reach in your lanes, cost per confirmed order, and how good the support is when a WhatsApp template gets rejected on a Friday evening. None of those are visible from a listing. Our guide to <a href="/blog/whatsapp-vs-sms-vs-ivr-cod-verification">sequencing WhatsApp, SMS and IVR</a> covers how to measure them.</p>

        <h2 id="divergence">The three differences that actually matter</h2>

        <p><strong>Where the order is captured.</strong> COD King's listing describes a replacement one-step COD form placed on product pages and the cart, with per-plan caps on how many orders can run through it — 60 on the free tier, 500, 10,000, then unlimited on the top tier. HillTeck's listing mentions pop-ups and a subscriber form, and its gallery includes a subscriber pop-up, but it does not describe a replacement COD order form. This is an architectural choice rather than a quality difference, and it has real consequences in both directions: a custom form lets you verify before an order exists and lets you put upsells and quantity offers in the one place a COD buyer is definitely looking, and it also means you have left Shopify's own checkout and taken on everything that goes with that. We wrote about that trade-off at length in <a href="/blog/native-checkout-vs-cod-order-form">native checkout versus a replacement COD form</a>, and the honest summary is that plenty of brands should stay in the native checkout.</p>

        <p><strong>What you can do to an order that looks risky.</strong> This is the widest gap. COD King's listing describes partial, advance or upfront payment on risky orders, flexible COD fees, prepaid discounts, hiding a payment method, and rules to limit COD by pincode, cart value, product or RTO risk. On the prepaid conversion route, HillTeck's site describes sending a payment link over WhatsApp with discounts and tailored incentives; neither its listing nor its site mentions partial or advance COD payment, a COD fee, or pincode-level COD rules. If your plan for a high-risk order is "ask for a small amount upfront and ship it anyway", that is the single question that settles this comparison, and our piece on <a href="/blog/partial-cod-payment-advance-amount">how much advance to collect</a> explains why that middle option exists at all. If your plan is "offer a discount to pay in full, otherwise cancel", both describe it.</p>

        <p><strong>How much lives downstream of the order.</strong> Here HillTeck is clearly the broader product. Campaigns and broadcasts, a shared inbox, a no-code chatbot, Instagram DM automation and WhatsApp commerce are all on its site; COD King's listing covers tracking messages and cart recovery but does not mention broadcast campaigns, a shared inbox, chatbots or Instagram. If you are also shopping for a retention and conversational-commerce tool, one vendor describes that and the other does not, and consolidating two bills into one is a legitimate reason to choose.</p>

        <p>One smaller difference worth naming because it bites in fulfilment: HillTeck's listing explicitly says a label is added to the Shopify order on confirmation or cancellation. COD King's listing does not mention order tagging anywhere. If your picking process, your courier allocation rules or your 3PL integration key off Shopify order tags, ask both vendors directly rather than inferring from a listing — this is precisely the kind of thing that exists but goes undocumented.</p>

        <h2 id="pricing">Pricing: two different shapes, not two different numbers</h2>

        <p>Neither app is priced in a way you can put side by side, and it is worth understanding why before you ask for quotes.</p>

        <p>COD King's listing publishes a structure. The free tier is free to install and charges 1.5% on partially paid orders, with the COD form capped at 60 orders. Three paid tiers are displayed at a nominal \$0.01 per month, which is not the real cost — the real cost is per-message rates by country plus the percentage charged on partially paid orders, which falls to 0.8%, then 0.5%, then nothing as you move up. The upper tiers add a local SMS gateway, branded SMS templates, the option to bring your own WhatsApp number at a stated per-message fee plus Meta's own charge, a Shopify Plus OTP extension at checkout, removal of COD King branding, and higher COD form caps. All charges are billed in USD and there is a seven-day free trial.</p>

        <p>HillTeck's Shopify listing shows one entry, "Free to install", with the note that additional charges may apply, and no plans, prices or quotas. Its own pricing page lists three tiers — Starter on a free trial, Growth described as usage-based, Enterprise described as custom — with no numeric prices, and states that exact per-call and per-message rates are available inside the Shopify app or on request.</p>

        <p>The practical consequence is that you cannot compare these on the app store, so do not try. Ask each vendor the same five questions: the per-message rate for each country you ship to, the per-call IVR rate separately from the messaging rate, whether you can connect your own WhatsApp Business number and what that costs, what percentage applies to any partially paid order, and what happens to your rate when volume triples in October. Then convert everything into one number — cost per confirmed order — because a cheaper per-message rate on a channel with worse reach is more expensive per confirmation, and the per-message rate is the one both the industry and your own finance team will fixate on.</p>

        <h2 id="ratings">What the ratings can and cannot tell you</h2>

        <p>Read on 21 November 2026, COD King's listing showed an average of 4.9 across 994 reviews, and HillTeck's showed 4.8 across 157. HillTeck's listing launched on 18 June 2020 and COD King's on 20 May 2021. Both figures move constantly, so treat them as a snapshot rather than a fact.</p>

        <p>They are also a weaker signal than they look. Review volume tracks install base and how persistently a vendor asks for reviews at least as much as it tracks quality, and 157 reviews at 4.8 is a good result, just a smaller sample. The genuinely useful reading is the low-star reviews on both listings: they tell you what breaks, how long it took to get fixed, and whether the answer was a human. Ten minutes in the one- and two-star reviews of both apps is worth more than any comparison table, including this one.</p>

        <p>Two other listing details are worth a look if they apply to you. COD King's listing carries Shopify's Built for Shopify badge and names nine interface languages, including Hindi, Arabic and Urdu; HillTeck's listing says English. And HillTeck's description says it verifies prepaid orders as well as COD, which matters if your address quality is poor across the board rather than only on cash orders — see <a href="/blog/address-quality-cod-orders">address quality for COD orders</a> for why that is often the real problem.</p>

        <p>A word on the headline outcome numbers both vendors publish. HillTeck's site leads with reducing RTO by 40%, and the auto-generated review summary on its Shopify listing mentions merchants reporting drops of up to 95%. Discount all of it, and discount ours the same way. RTO outcomes depend on your category, your average order value, your lanes, your dispatch speed and how aggressively you were already filtering orders. No app produces a percentage that transfers to your store, and any vendor quoting you one without asking about your category is guessing.</p>

        <h2 id="hillteck-better">Where HillTeck is the better pick</h2>

        <p>Four situations, and they are not edge cases.</p>

        <p>If you want one vendor for both COD verification and WhatsApp marketing, HillTeck describes more of the second job than we do. Running a verification app plus a separate marketing platform means two bills, two integrations and two places to check when a message did not go out, and avoiding that is worth real money.</p>

        <p>If you want to lead with voice, both its listing and its site put IVR first, and a vendor whose product is organised around the channel you care about most tends to have thought harder about it.</p>

        <p>If your fulfilment process depends on Shopify order tags, its listing commits to writing a label on confirmation or cancellation in plain text. Ours does not mention tagging.</p>

        <p>And if you want conversational support alongside verification — a shared inbox, a chatbot, Instagram DMs in the same place — that is on its site and not on ours.</p>

        <h2 id="wrong">When COD King is the wrong choice</h2>

        <p>Several cases, and we would rather you worked them out now than after installing.</p>

        <p><strong>Your RTO is not a COD intent problem.</strong> Verification converts a refusal at the door into a cancellation before dispatch, which is cheaper but is not the same as a delivery. If your returns are driven by sizing, damage in transit or orders that arrive two weeks late, no verification app helps; <a href="/blog/apparel-sizing-cod-returns">sizing</a> and <a href="/blog/dispatch-speed-cod-orders">dispatch speed</a> are the fixes, and an app that confirms intent will just confirm intent on orders that were always going to come back.</p>

        <p><strong>Your volume is small.</strong> At a handful of COD orders a day, a person calling customers is cheaper, warmer and more informative than any automation, and you will learn more from ten conversations than from a dashboard.</p>

        <p><strong>You want a full WhatsApp marketing suite.</strong> Broadcasts, segmented campaigns, chatbots, a shared inbox and Instagram automation are not what our listing describes. Buying us for that is buying the wrong tool.</p>

        <p><strong>You need to stay entirely inside Shopify's native checkout.</strong> Our COD form is a replacement surface, and the checkout-level OTP extension sits on the top tier and on Shopify Plus. If leaving the native checkout is off the table for you and Plus is not on the cards, a good part of what makes us distinctive is unavailable to you.</p>

        <p><strong>Nobody will action the output.</strong> Flags, risk scores and COD rules only pay for themselves if someone changes a courier, a payment option or a pincode policy in response. If no one owns that, the app is a line item. <a href="/blog/cod-risk-scoring">Risk scoring</a> is only as good as the three actions attached to it.</p>

        <p>Our longer piece on <a href="/blog/best-cod-verification-apps-shopify">how to choose a COD verification app</a> runs the same test across the wider field, including options not mentioned here.</p>

        <h2 id="decide">How to decide in two weeks</h2>

        <p>Both have free entry points, so test rather than argue. Run one app for a week on a defined slice of orders, then the other for a week on a comparable slice. Do not run both at once on the same cohort; you will not be able to attribute anything.</p>

        <p>Record four numbers each week. The share of orders where the customer was actually reached. The share that reached a confirmed state. The median time from order to confirmation, because that is what decides whether verification sits on your dispatch critical path. And total channel spend divided by confirmed orders.</p>

        <p>Then answer the question that actually settles it: of the orders that did not confirm, what do you want to happen? If the answer is "cancel them" in both cases, choose on reach, cost and support, and either app will serve you. If the answer involves collecting a part payment, charging a COD fee, or switching a pincode to prepaid-only, then one of these products describes that and the other does not, and the decision is already made.</p>

        <h2 id="short">The short version</h2>

        <p>Both apps verify COD orders over OTP, WhatsApp and IVR, recover abandoned carts and push buyers to prepaid, and on that common ground the listings will not separate them. HillTeck is the broader messaging platform: campaigns, broadcasts, chatbot, inbox, Instagram, plus explicit order labelling in Shopify, with pricing published as usage-based tiers carrying no public numbers. COD King is organised around the payment decision: a replacement COD form, partial and advance payment, COD fees, prepaid discounts and COD rules by pincode, cart value, product or risk, with a published fee structure and per-message rates you have to ask for. If you need a marketing suite, that points one way. If you need a middle option between shipping a risky COD order and cancelling it, that points the other. Test both for a week each, measure cost per confirmed order, and read the low-star reviews before you read either vendor's RTO claims — including ours.</p>
