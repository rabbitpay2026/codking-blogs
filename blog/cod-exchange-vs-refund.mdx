      <div className="ck-summary"><div className="ck-summary-label">Summary</div><p>A prepaid refund is a reversal: the money goes back down the rail it came up. A COD refund is an outbound payment to someone you have no payment instrument for, funded from cash the courier collected and remitted weeks later. That asymmetry is why exchange-first policies work better on COD than on prepaid, why store credit is tempting and easy to overuse, and why the operational work — collecting payout details, coding return reasons, evidencing the unit before pickup, deciding who pays each freight leg — has to be done before the requests start arriving. This is a guide to building the three exits deliberately, pricing each one, and measuring whether your policy is retaining revenue or just moving the loss around.</p></div>

      <div className="ck-body">
        <p className="ck-lede">Almost everything written about COD returns is about the parcel that never got delivered. This article is about the harder case: the parcel that did get delivered, the buyer paid cash at the door, and now they want to send it back. Operationally this is a different problem from an RTO, and the difference is not the logistics — reverse pickup works the same either way. The difference is that on a prepaid order the refund path already exists, and on a COD order it does not.</p>

        <h2 id="two-questions">Two questions, not one</h2>

        <p>"Exchange or refund" is usually discussed as a single policy choice, as though it were a dial between generous and strict. It is actually two separate questions, and conflating them is why so many returns policies read well and execute badly.</p>

        <p><strong>The first question is what the buyer gets.</strong> The same product in a different size or variant, a fresh unit of the same thing, store credit, or their money. That is a commercial and customer-experience decision.</p>

        <p><strong>The second question is how the money moves.</strong> For three of those four answers, it does not move at all — which is precisely why they are cheaper. For the fourth, you are making an outbound payment to a person whose only prior transaction with you was handing cash to a delivery agent. You have no card on file, no payment token, no mandate, and no instrument to reverse.</p>

        <p>Brands that run COD well separate these questions and solve the second one first. If you cannot execute a refund cleanly within a few days, then every element of your policy that promises one is a support ticket waiting to happen, regardless of how reasonable the policy sounds.</p>

        <h2 id="money">Where the money actually is</h2>

        <p>Trace a COD rupee before you design anything. The buyer pays the delivery agent. The courier holds that collection, nets its charges, and remits to your bank on its own cycle — a lag that is the entire subject of <a href="/blog/cod-remittance-cycle-cash-flow">COD remittance cycles and cash flow</a>. By the time a return request arrives, the money is usually in your account, already mixed with everything else.</p>

        <p>Three consequences follow, and all of them are easy to miss until the first awkward case.</p>

        <p><strong>You cannot reverse the collection.</strong> There is no transaction to void. A refund is a new, outbound payment, and it needs a destination you do not yet have.</p>

        <p><strong>The timing can run against you.</strong> If a buyer returns quickly on a slow remittance cycle, you may be asked to refund money the courier has not yet paid you. For a single order that is noise. Through a festive peak, when both order volume and return volume are elevated at once, it is a working-capital event worth anticipating.</p>

        <p><strong>Shopify will not do it for you.</strong> A manual-payment order has no gateway behind it, so marking a refund in the admin records the accounting entry and adjusts the order, but moves no money. Someone has to make the actual payment, and if nobody owns that step it silently does not happen. The order looks refunded in your reports and the buyer is still waiting.</p>

        <p>That third point is the one that produces the worst outcomes, because it fails quietly and the dashboard says everything is fine.</p>

        <h2 id="rails">Build the payout rail before you need it</h2>

        <p>There are only a few realistic ways to pay a COD buyer back, and the right move is to pick one as primary, one as fallback, and document both.</p>

        <p><strong>UPI to a collected identifier.</strong> For most Indian D2C brands this is the practical primary: fast, cheap, and the buyer already has it. The constraint is that you need the identifier, and the time to ask for it is during the return request, not after you have approved it.</p>

        <p><strong>Bank transfer to account details.</strong> The universal fallback, and the one to use when a buyer cannot or will not give a UPI identifier. It asks more of the buyer, takes longer, and is more prone to keying errors, so it should not be the default path if you can avoid it.</p>

        <p><strong>A payment-gateway payout or refund-link product.</strong> Several Indian payment processors offer a facility where you send the buyer a link and they choose where the money lands. It removes the data-collection burden from your team and the keying risk from the transfer. Whether it is worth the per-transaction cost depends on your return volume; check what your own processor actually offers rather than assuming, because these products change.</p>

        <p><strong>Store credit or a coupon.</strong> Not a refund, though it is often presented as one. Covered separately below, because the failure modes are different.</p>

        <p>Whichever you pick, three details decide whether this runs smoothly or generates disputes. Ask for payout details in the return request form itself, as a required field, so no case ever reaches approval without them. Have one named person or role responsible for executing the payment and reconciling it against the order, because this is the step that goes missing. And confirm the payment outward to the buyer with a reference number — a refund the buyer cannot see is, from their side, a refund that did not happen, and it generates a second round of support contact that costs more than the refund did.</p>

        <h2 id="default">Make exchange the default exit, and make it feel like one</h2>

        <p>Once the money asymmetry is clear, the case for exchange-first is obvious: an exchange keeps the revenue, keeps the customer, and requires no payout rail at all. The question is how to make it the default without making it the only option, which is where most policies go wrong.</p>

        <p>The difference between an exchange-first policy that works and one that annoys people is almost entirely about friction symmetry. If the exchange path is one tap and the refund path requires an email, a wait and a follow-up, buyers notice, and the brands that do this collect reviews that say so. If the exchange path is one tap and the refund path is two, you have expressed a preference without obstructing anyone.</p>

        <p>What genuinely moves buyers toward exchange, in rough order of effect:</p>

        <p><strong>Speed.</strong> An exchange that dispatches the replacement before the original unit comes back is dramatically more attractive than one that waits for reverse pickup, QC and restock. This is the single biggest lever, and it costs you the risk of the original unit never arriving — which for a buyer with a delivery history is a small risk, and is why this should be a rule keyed to order history rather than a blanket offer.</p>

        <p><strong>Certainty about stock.</strong> "Exchange for a different size" is only appealing if the size is available. Showing live availability inside the return flow converts; offering an exchange and then discovering the variant is out of stock is worse than not offering it.</p>

        <p><strong>Free freight on the exchange leg.</strong> Covered below, but as a rule: if you charge for the exchange leg and not for the refund, you have inverted your own incentive.</p>

        <p><strong>A small upgrade allowance.</strong> Letting the buyer exchange into something of slightly higher value by paying the difference turns a return into a cross-sell. It requires you to collect a top-up payment, which is a prepaid transaction and therefore easy.</p>

        <p>What does not work is the thing most brands reach for first: refusing refunds outright, or burying them in policy language. On a COD order the buyer has already demonstrated that they are willing to transact with a brand they did not fully trust. A brand that then makes getting money back difficult confirms the caution, and the cost of that reputation lands on your whole COD funnel, not on the one order.</p>

        <h2 id="credit">Store credit: useful, and easy to overuse</h2>

        <p>Store credit sits between exchange and refund. It solves your payout problem completely, it retains the revenue, and buyers often accept it. It is also the option most capable of damaging trust when it is handled as a substitute for a refund rather than an alternative to one.</p>

        <p>The distinction is consent. Credit offered as a choice, with a small sweetener for taking it and a refund still available, is a good deal for both sides: the buyer gets value immediately instead of waiting for a transfer, and you keep the cash. Credit issued because you would rather not pay out is a worse product than a refund sold as a better one, and buyers can tell.</p>

        <p>If you offer it, make the terms unambiguous. State whether it expires and give any expiry enough room to be genuinely usable. State whether it can be combined with discounts, because discovering that it cannot, at checkout, is the moment the goodwill evaporates. Make the balance visible to the buyer without having to ask. And keep the credit liability somewhere you can see it, because unredeemed credit is a real obligation that tends to be invisible in standard reporting until it is large.</p>

        <h2 id="reason">Code the reason before you choose the exit</h2>

        <p>The exit should follow from the return reason, by rule. Deciding case by case is slow, inconsistent between staff, and destroys the data you need to reduce returns in the first place.</p>

        <p>A workable reason set is short and physically distinguishable, in the same spirit as the grading scale in <a href="/blog/cod-returns-qc-restocking">returns QC and restocking</a>:</p>

        <p><strong>Wrong size or fit.</strong> The natural exchange case, and in apparel the dominant one. Exchange should be the headline option, with the size recommendation captured so the data feeds back into your <a href="/blog/apparel-sizing-cod-returns">sizing content</a>.</p>

        <p><strong>Wrong item or variant shipped.</strong> Your error. Replacement, dispatched immediately, both freight legs on you, and no inspection gate in front of it. Anything slower converts a picking mistake into a lost customer.</p>

        <p><strong>Damaged or defective on arrival.</strong> Replacement or refund at the buyer's choice, and the one case where you should not push exchange. Also the case that needs photo evidence most, both to decide a courier claim and to spot the SKU-level pattern.</p>

        <p><strong>Not as described.</strong> Treat it as a listing problem rather than a buyer problem. Refund without friction, and route the reason to whoever owns the product page, because repeat instances on one SKU are cheaper to fix on the page than in the warehouse.</p>

        <p><strong>Changed mind.</strong> The one case where your policy is genuinely discretionary, and where exchange or credit is a fair default with a refund available within the stated window.</p>

        <p>Record the reason against the order, not just the SKU. Reason tied to the order is what later lets you cut returns by courier, by lane, by acquisition source and by whether the order was COD or prepaid — and that last cut is frequently the most interesting one, because a return-rate gap between COD and prepaid buyers on the same SKU is telling you something about intent, not about the product.</p>

        <h2 id="freight">Who pays for the second leg</h2>

        <p>An exchange involves two freight movements beyond the original delivery: the unit coming back, and the new unit going out. A refund involves one. Someone pays for these, and pretending otherwise is how exchange programmes quietly become loss-makers.</p>

        <p>The defensible split, which also happens to point buyers the way you want them to go:</p>

        <p><strong>Your error — wrong item, defect, damage.</strong> You pay both legs, with no exceptions and no inspection gate in front of the replacement.</p>

        <p><strong>Fit or variant exchange.</strong> Absorb the legs if your margin allows it, because this is the exit you want chosen and freight is the cheapest incentive available. If margin does not allow it, charge a flat, stated exchange fee rather than actual freight, which is simpler to communicate and does not punish distant buyers.</p>

        <p><strong>Changed mind with a refund.</strong> A reasonable place to pass on the reverse freight, stated clearly up front. Not a penalty, just the honest cost of the movement.</p>

        <p>Work out the real per-movement cost before setting any of these, using the same build-up as <a href="/blog/cod-return-cost-reverse-logistics">what a COD return actually costs</a>: freight, packaging, handling and the QC time, not just the courier's reverse rate card. Brands routinely set an exchange fee below their own cost per movement and then wonder why a rising exchange rate is not improving anything.</p>

        <h2 id="windows">Windows, and the clock buyers actually measure</h2>

        <p>Two timers matter and brands tend to publish only the first.</p>

        <p>The <strong>request window</strong> is how long after delivery a buyer may raise a return. This is a policy number, it should be stated plainly, and on COD it is worth being slightly more generous than you would be on prepaid, because the COD buyer took the leap of ordering from you in the first place.</p>

        <p>The <strong>resolution clock</strong> is how long you take from request to resolved — replacement delivered, or money in the buyer's account. This is the number buyers actually experience and almost nobody commits to. Commit to it internally even if you do not publish it, measure the distribution rather than the average, and look at the tail: a policy where most refunds land in three days and some take three weeks is remembered for the three weeks.</p>

        <p>The tail is also where the payout-rail work pays off. Refunds that stall almost always stall on a missing detail, a failed transfer nobody retried, or an unassigned payment step — not on the decision.</p>

        <h2 id="category">Where the defaults should differ by category</h2>

        <p>One policy across a mixed catalogue will be wrong somewhere. The variable that matters is whether the returned unit can be sold again.</p>

        <p><strong>Apparel and footwear.</strong> Exchange-first is natural because the dominant reason is fit and the unit is usually resaleable. This is the category where a fast, generous exchange flow genuinely changes your economics.</p>

        <p><strong>Electronics and accessories.</strong> Replacement-first for defects, refund available, and watch sealed-unit condition carefully — an opened unit may not be resaleable as new regardless of how it looks.</p>

        <p><strong>Cosmetics, personal care, consumables.</strong> No resale after opening, so exchange has little value to you and the honest policy is a tight window, a clear non-returnable rule for opened items, and a refund or credit where you accept the return at all. Do not offer an exchange you will fulfil from new stock while writing off the old unit; that is the worst of both options.</p>

        <p><strong>High-value items.</strong> The exit matters less than the inspection. Evidence the unit thoroughly before pickup, and consider making these prepaid-only at checkout instead, which is a question of <a href="/blog/cod-risk-scoring">COD risk rules</a> rather than returns policy.</p>

        <h2 id="measure">What to measure</h2>

        <p>Four numbers tell you whether the policy is working, and they have to be read together, because each one on its own can be improved in ways that make the business worse.</p>

        <p><strong>Exit mix.</strong> The split of resolved requests across exchange, replacement, credit and refund. Rising exchange share is the goal, but only alongside the next number.</p>

        <p><strong>Resolution time by exit, at the tail.</strong> The ninetieth percentile, not the mean. If exchange share is rising while exchange resolution time is also rising, you are pushing people into a path you cannot service.</p>

        <p><strong>Net revenue retained per return request.</strong> Value kept after freight, handling and any write-down of the returned unit. This is the number that tells you whether an exchange is actually better than a refund for a given category, and occasionally it says no.</p>

        <p><strong>Repeat purchase rate after a return.</strong> The one that catches a policy that looks efficient and is quietly costing you customers. A buyer who had a return handled well is often worth more than one who never needed to return anything, and if your retained-revenue number is improving while this one falls, the policy is harvesting rather than working.</p>

        <p>Cut all four by COD against prepaid. If COD buyers return more but also accept exchanges more readily, your exchange investment is well placed. If they return more and refund more, the problem is upstream of returns — in intent at the point of order, not in policy after delivery.</p>

        <h2 id="where">Where COD King fits, and where it does not</h2>

        <p>COD King works before dispatch. It confirms order intent, applies COD rules by pincode, cart value and customer history, and collects a partial advance where the risk justifies it — which reduces the number of orders that reach a doorstep without real intent behind them. A partial advance also leaves you with a prepaid element you can refund down the rail it came up, which is a quietly useful side effect.</p>

        <p>What it does not do is run your post-delivery returns desk. The exits, the payout rail, the reason codes and the freight splits in this article are yours to build, and they matter most for the orders that did convert and did deliver.</p>

        <h2 id="short">The short version</h2>

        <p>On a delivered COD order there is nothing to reverse, so a refund is an outbound payment to someone you have no instrument for. Build the payout rail first — a primary method, a fallback, payout details collected as a required field in the return request, one named owner for executing and reconciling the payment, and an outward confirmation to the buyer. Then make exchange the default by making it faster and cheaper than the alternatives rather than by making refunds hard, because a COD buyer who finds it difficult to get money back will tell other people. Offer store credit as a choice with a sweetener, never as a substitute. Let coded return reasons pick the exit so nobody decides case by case, and put the freight on yourself wherever the fault is yours. Publish the request window, commit internally to a resolution clock, and watch its tail. Then read exit mix, resolution time, retained revenue and post-return repeat rate together — and split every one of them by COD against prepaid, because that comparison usually points at a problem that lives upstream of your returns policy entirely.</p>
