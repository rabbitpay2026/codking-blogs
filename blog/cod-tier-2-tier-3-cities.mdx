      <div className="ck-summary"><div className="ck-summary-label">Summary</div><p>For most Indian D2C brands the majority of COD volume is already outside the metros, and the metro playbook does not transfer. The tier label itself is not the variable that matters — it is a proxy for three things that do: more hops between pickup and doorstep, a last leg often run by a franchise rather than the courier, and NDR data that arrives later and says less. Fix the lane, not the tier. That means allocating on coverage depth before rate, treating the pickup cut-off as a hard deadline rather than a target, writing addresses the way local riders actually navigate, and pricing COD policy on delivered outcomes per pincode cluster instead of on a city label.</p></div>

      <div className="ck-body">
        <p className="ck-lede">Most brands meet tier-3 as a line in the RTO report they cannot explain. Delivery percentage is fine in Mumbai, Bengaluru and the NCR, acceptable in Jaipur and Coimbatore, and then there is a long tail of pincodes where a quarter or a third of cash orders come back and nobody can say why. The instinct is to turn COD off there. That is usually the wrong move, because the tail is no longer a tail.</p>

        <p>Logistics software firm ClickPost, reporting on 42.5 million shipments across the Diwali 2025 season on its network, said 50.7% of orders went to tier-3 cities and 24.8% to tier-2. On a panel of around 200 D2C brands over 9 to 13 October 2026 it put tier-3 at 45% of shipments, tier-2 at 22% and metro plus tier-1 at 33%. E-commerce software firm Unicommerce, reporting on its first festive week of 2026 from 7 to 13 October, put tier-3 at 42% of order volume. The datasets are different and the tier definitions are each company's own, so the numbers should not be averaged — but they point the same way. Upcountry is not an expansion opportunity you will get to next year. It is where your parcels are already going.</p>

        <p>ClickPost also reported that 52% of tier-3 orders in its Diwali 2025 dataset were cash on delivery. So the lanes with your weakest operational visibility are also the lanes most exposed to cash.</p>

        <h2 id="proxy">The tier label is a proxy, and a bad one</h2>

        <p>There is no official tier classification for logistics. Every company draws its own, usually off population or off its own hub map, which is why two reports on the same month can disagree by ten points. More importantly, the label does not describe the thing that breaks.</p>

        <p>Two pincodes can both be "tier-3" and behave nothing alike. One sits twenty minutes from a courier's own branch in a district headquarters and delivers at metro-grade rates. The other is forty kilometres further out, flagged as out-of-delivery-area, served twice a week by an agent who covers six villages, and fails a third of cash orders. Averaging them produces a number that describes neither and justifies no decision.</p>

        <p>What actually varies, in order of impact:</p>

        <p><strong>Hop count.</strong> A metro parcel typically moves pickup, hub, delivery hub, doorstep. An upcountry parcel adds a line haul and a regional sort, and often a franchise or agent leg at the end. Every hop adds a day of exposure and a place for the parcel to sit.</p>

        <p><strong>Who makes the final attempt.</strong> In metros it is usually the courier's own rider on the courier's own app. Outside them it is frequently a franchisee or a contracted agent, paid per delivery, covering a wide area on a fixed round. That changes the economics of a second attempt, and it changes what you can ask for.</p>

        <p><strong>Attempt budget and its elasticity.</strong> A second attempt in a dense urban beat costs the courier very little. A second attempt on a round that goes out twice a week costs a slot that could have carried another parcel. Couriers price and ration accordingly, whatever the service agreement says about three attempts.</p>

        <p>Everything else in this article follows from those three.</p>

        <h2 id="hops">Dispatch speed stops being a nice-to-have</h2>

        <p>Our guide to <a href="/blog/dispatch-speed-cod-orders">dispatch speed</a> makes the general case that the hours between order and manifest matter more than most teams think. On upcountry lanes the case is sharper, because the penalty for missing a cut-off is not a few hours — it is a line haul.</p>

        <p>A parcel manifested twenty minutes after the evening pickup in a metro lane usually goes out the next morning and arrives a day late. The same miss on a lane whose line haul departs once a day, feeding a regional sort that feeds a twice-weekly round, can cost three or four days. Order-to-doorstep stretches, and the buyer's recollection of why they ordered stretches with it. Cancellation and refusal risk rise with that interval, which is the mechanism behind most of the "tier-3 buyers are flaky" folklore. Often they are not flaky; they have simply been waiting nine days.</p>

        <p>Two concrete consequences. First, verification must not sit on the critical path for these lanes. If a confirmation call takes four hours and that pushes the parcel past the cut-off, the verification has cost more delivery percentage than it saved. Confirm in parallel with picking, and set a hard rule about what happens to an unconfirmed order at cut-off time. Second, your dispatch service level should be set per lane group, not per store. A same-day manifest target that you hit 80% of the time is fine for a metro beat and expensive on a once-daily line haul.</p>

        <h2 id="address">Addresses that are directions, not addresses</h2>

        <p>Outside the metros, the address field frequently contains navigation rather than an address: a temple, a shop, a bus stop, a person's name, "behind the old petrol pump". This is not a data quality failure on the buyer's part. In many places it is how the location is actually found, and the formal address would be less useful to the rider than the landmark.</p>

        <p>The mistake is to treat these as malformed and let your validation reject or silently truncate them. The fixes are mechanical:</p>

        <p><strong>Give the landmark its own field</strong> and make it prominent for pincodes you have classified as upcountry. A separate field survives into the shipping label; a landmark buried at the end of a 180-character address line often does not, because label templates truncate.</p>

        <p><strong>Check your label output, not your order data.</strong> Read a few printed labels for these lanes. Many courier integrations cap address line length well below what Shopify accepts, and the part that gets cut is the end — which is exactly where the landmark sits.</p>

        <p><strong>Validate the pincode against the locality, and stop there.</strong> Pincode-to-city consistency and a plausible phone shape are worth enforcing. Minimum word counts and street-number requirements are not; they fail honest addresses in small towns far more often than they catch bad ones.</p>

        <p><strong>Capture a second number where you can.</strong> On a franchise last leg, an unreachable phone is close to fatal, because the agent will not make a speculative trip. One alternate number recovers more upcountry NDRs than almost anything else you can add to the form.</p>

        <p>The general method for scoring and repairing addresses before packing is in <a href="/blog/address-quality-cod-orders">address quality for COD orders</a>. The upcountry-specific point is that the rules you enforce in a metro form will reject a correct small-town address, and you will never see the orders you lost.</p>

        <h2 id="courier">Allocate on coverage depth, then on rate</h2>

        <p>Courier rate cards converge; coverage depth does not. For a given upcountry pincode one courier may deliver with its own staff, another through a franchise, and a third will accept the shipment and then transfer it to a local partner — which is slower, less visible and usually worse on cash. All three will show the pincode as serviceable.</p>

        <p>So the question to ask a courier is not "do you cover this pincode" but "who makes the final attempt there, how often does the round go out, and is there an out-of-delivery-area surcharge". Those three answers predict delivery percentage better than the rate card does.</p>

        <p>In practice, build the allocation table from your own delivered outcomes, as described in <a href="/blog/courier-allocation-cod-orders">courier allocation for COD orders</a>, but group the rows differently. A per-pincode table is too sparse upcountry — many pincodes will have five lifetime orders, and five orders tell you nothing. Group by the courier's own branch or service area where you can get it, or failing that by the first three or four digits of the pincode, which approximates a sorting region. Those buckets accumulate enough volume to be read.</p>

        <p>Keep a minority split running on every lane group you care about. Without it you cannot tell whether a bad lane is bad or whether your chosen courier is bad at it, and that distinction decides whether you fix the courier or restrict the lane.</p>

        <h2 id="ndr">The NDR is thinner, and it arrives later</h2>

        <p>Upcountry NDR data degrades in three ways at once. The reason code is more likely to be a generic default, because the person entering it is further from your courier's quality systems. The timestamp is less reliable, so an attempt logged today may have happened yesterday. And the window you have to respond is compressed relative to the next round — if the agent goes out on Tuesday and Friday and the NDR lands on Wednesday morning, you have two days to resolve something, not two hours.</p>

        <p>That changes how the queue should be worked. The general triage method is in <a href="/blog/ndr-management-cod-orders">NDR management</a>; three adjustments for these lanes:</p>

        <p><strong>Work upcountry NDRs first, not last.</strong> Most teams work the queue in arrival order, which effectively prioritises metro NDRs because they arrive faster and in volume. Invert it for lanes with infrequent rounds — those are the ones where missing a cycle converts a recoverable NDR into an RTO.</p>

        <p><strong>Resolve to an action the agent can take without you.</strong> "Customer confirms, please reattempt" is weak on a franchise leg. A corrected landmark, an alternate number, and a stated delivery window the buyer has agreed to are things the agent can act on during a round they were making anyway.</p>

        <p><strong>Do not read the reason codes literally.</strong> On these lanes, "customer not available" and "customer refused" are frequently the same underlying event — the agent could not find or reach the buyer and closed the attempt with whatever code is fastest. Treat the split as unreliable upcountry and lean on your own confirmation data instead.</p>

        <h2 id="reach">Reaching the buyer at all</h2>

        <p>Confirmation economics shift outside the metros. Deliverability of any single channel is lower, and the window is longer because the parcel takes longer anyway. The practical implication is to stop thinking in terms of one channel and start thinking in terms of a ladder with a deadline: a message first, a second channel if nothing comes back within a set number of hours, and a call only for the orders that justify the cost — which upcountry means the higher-value ones and those in lanes with poor history.</p>

        <p>Language matters more than brands expect. A confirmation message in English to a buyer in a small town may be read and not understood well enough to act on, which looks identical in your data to a message that was ignored. If your catalogue sells into specific regions, a regional-language variant of the confirmation template is a cheap test with a measurable outcome. The channel trade-offs themselves — reach, cost, consent and what each one is actually good at — are covered in <a href="/blog/whatsapp-vs-sms-vs-ivr-cod-verification">WhatsApp vs SMS vs IVR</a>.</p>

        <p>One thing not to do: do not extend the confirmation window because upcountry buyers are slower to reply. You will push the parcel past the cut-off and lose more to transit delay than you gain in confirmation rate. Keep the deadline and change the channel mix instead.</p>

        <h2 id="policy">Policy by lane, not by tier</h2>

        <p>Once you have lane groups with enough volume to read, the policy decisions become arithmetic rather than argument. Four treatments, in increasing severity, each triggered by delivered-order outcomes in that group rather than by the city label:</p>

        <p><strong>Leave it open.</strong> Most upcountry lanes earn their place. Delivery percentage is a few points below your metro average and the contribution per delivered order still clears your cost of a failure. Do nothing.</p>

        <p><strong>Cap the cart value.</strong> The cheapest intervention and the most under-used. A lane that delivers reliably at ₹700 and poorly at ₹3,500 does not need COD switched off; it needs a ceiling above which COD is unavailable. This works because the cost of a failure is roughly flat in freight terms but the capital at risk scales with order value.</p>

        <p><strong>Require an advance or a fee.</strong> A part payment turns a refusal from free into costly for the buyer, and it is the right tool for lanes where intent rather than reachability is the problem. How much to ask for, and why the amount should be flat rather than a percentage, is in <a href="/blog/partial-cod-payment-advance-amount">how much COD advance to collect</a>.</p>

        <p><strong>Prepaid only.</strong> Reserve this for lane groups where the arithmetic is clearly negative across enough orders to be confident, and revisit it quarterly — courier coverage upcountry changes faster than anywhere else, and a lane you closed in March may be served by a new branch by October. A closed lane that nobody re-examines is lost revenue that compounds.</p>

        <p>Target prepaid incentives at these lanes rather than sitewide. The value of converting a COD order to prepaid is the failure rate multiplied by the cost of a failure, and both are higher upcountry — so the same discount buys more there. The full arithmetic, including why a sitewide percentage offer usually loses money, is in <a href="/blog/prepaid-incentive-design">designing a prepaid incentive</a>. The practical point is that a lane-targeted offer clears the break-even bar in places a sitewide one never will, because the right-hand side of that calculation is largest exactly where delivery is weakest.</p>

        <h2 id="reverse">The return leg is where it actually hurts</h2>

        <p>A failed upcountry delivery is more expensive than a failed metro delivery for a reason that has nothing to do with the forward rate: the parcel retraces every hop. Five hops out, five hops back, often with a holding period at the branch before the return is even manifested. That means more days of working capital tied up, more handling, and a higher chance the unit arrives back damaged or unsellable.</p>

        <p>If you load a single blended cost of failure onto every order, you are understating the upcountry number and overstating the metro one — which systematically biases every policy decision you make from it. Build the per-parcel figure properly, as in <a href="/blog/cod-return-cost-reverse-logistics">what a COD return actually costs you</a>, and then compute it at least twice: once for metro lanes and once for upcountry. The gap is usually large enough to change which lanes you would cap.</p>

        <p>Two smaller things worth doing. Set a rule for the lanes where the return freight plus handling exceeds the recoverable value of the unit, and dispose or liquidate locally rather than shipping it back; low-value, high-bulk SKUs on long lanes hit this threshold more often than people check. And read your return transit time by lane group, because a return sitting at a branch for two weeks is capital you have not accounted for and inventory your system thinks it has.</p>

        <h2 id="measure">Build the table once, then maintain it</h2>

        <p>Everything above runs off one artefact: a table of lane groups with delivered outcomes. If you build nothing else from this article, build this.</p>

        <p>One row per lane group — courier service area if you can get it, otherwise the first three or four digits of the pincode. Columns: shipped orders, delivered, NDR count, RTO count, average order value, COD share, average order-to-delivery days, and return transit days. Restrict it to a trailing window long enough to accumulate volume and short enough to still be true; a quarter is a reasonable default outside festive periods. Drop rows below a minimum order count rather than reading noise, and keep a separate pile for the lanes that are too sparse to judge — those get your default policy, not a bespoke one.</p>

        <p>Then add the only two derived columns that matter: contribution per shipped order, after loading that lane group's own cost of failure onto the failures; and the delivery percentage gap against your best-performing courier in the same group, which tells you whether the problem is the lane or the allocation.</p>

        <p>Review it monthly during normal trading and freeze policy changes before a festive peak rather than during one, for the reasons in <a href="/blog/festive-season-cod-operations">running COD through a festive peak</a>. Compare against your own prior quarter, not against anyone's published benchmark — the tier definitions differ, the category mixes differ, and the comparison will mislead you. That argument is made at length in <a href="/blog/rto-benchmarks-india">RTO benchmarks for Indian D2C</a>.</p>

        <h2 id="wrong">Three ways brands get this wrong</h2>

        <p><strong>Closing the tail wholesale.</strong> Switching COD off for everything outside a list of cities removes a large share of revenue to fix a problem concentrated in a small subset of lanes. Cap and gate before you close.</p>

        <p><strong>Judging lanes on too little data.</strong> A pincode with six orders and two returns is not a 33% RTO lane; it is a lane you know nothing about. Grouping is not a convenience, it is what makes the number mean anything.</p>

        <p><strong>Treating it as a buyer problem.</strong> "Tier-3 customers refuse more" is a conclusion that forecloses every available fix. The measurable drivers — transit days, hop count, who makes the attempt, whether the landmark survived onto the label, whether anyone could reach the buyer — are all things you control or can negotiate.</p>

        <p>COD King sits on the policy side of this: COD availability, caps, fees and advance collection applied as rules by pincode, cart value and order history rather than as one sitewide setting, which is what makes lane-level treatment possible without custom development. The confirmation ladder described above runs on the same rules.</p>

        <h2 id="short">The short version</h2>

        <p>Most of your COD volume is already outside the metros, and the tier label is a proxy for three things that actually matter: hop count, who makes the final attempt, and how often the round goes out. Treat the pickup cut-off as a hard deadline on long lanes, because a miss costs a line haul rather than an afternoon, and keep verification off the critical path. Let addresses be directions — a landmark field, and labels you have actually read — and capture a second phone number. Allocate couriers on coverage depth and your own delivered outcomes, grouped by service area rather than per pincode. Work upcountry NDRs first and resolve them into something a franchise agent can act on without calling you. Compute the cost of a failure separately for these lanes, because the parcel comes back the long way. Then cap and gate by lane group rather than closing the tail, and re-examine the closed lanes every quarter, because coverage upcountry is the fastest-moving thing in your operation.</p>
