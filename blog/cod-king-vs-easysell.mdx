      <div className="ck-summary"><div className="ck-summary-label">Summary</div><p>These two apps agree on the big architectural decision — replace Shopify's checkout with a one-step cash on delivery form — and then diverge on what that form is for. EasySell's listing is organised around average order value: upsells, downsells, bundles, cross-sell and a product recommender. COD King's is organised around the payment decision: partial COD, COD fees, prepaid discounts and rules by pincode. They also price on opposite models, which changes which one is cheaper entirely as a function of your order volume. Everything factual below was read from each listing on 25 November 2026.</p></div>

      <div className="ck-body">
        <p className="ck-lede">Most comparisons between COD form apps are useless because they compare feature checklists, and every app in this category ticks most of the same boxes. The useful question is narrower: these two have made the same bet about where a COD order gets captured, and then placed different bets about what happens next. Knowing which of those second bets matches your operation settles the choice in about ten minutes.</p>

        <p>A note on method first. Everything factual below comes from each app's own Shopify App Store listing, read on 25 November 2026, plus the vendors' own sites. Where a listing does not describe something, this article says exactly that rather than claiming the app cannot do it — listings are marketing pages, not specifications, and shipped features routinely never make it onto them. Prices, plans and ratings on both listings change. And because one of these apps is ours, there is a section below on where it is the wrong choice; read that one first if you are short on time.</p>

        <p>One housekeeping point. The app is listed as EasySell COD Form & Upsells and published by Tyslo FZE-LLC, and the vendor's own site at tyslo.com presents the same product as "Tyslo COD Form & Upsells", linking back to the EasySell listing. Same product, two names. Also worth noting: COD King's own listing names "Easy sell" in its "Works with" list, so the two are not mutually exclusive by design.</p>

        <h2 id="same-bet">The bet they share</h2>

        <p>Both apps replace Shopify's native checkout, for COD orders, with a form the merchant controls. EasySell's listing describes a one-click, mobile-first COD order form built in a drag-and-drop editor, with custom fields, custom layouts, templates and address autocomplete. COD King's describes a one-step COD form on product pages and the cart. Same structural move.</p>

        <p>That shared decision carries consequences neither vendor will put on a listing, and they apply equally to both. You leave Shopify's checkout, which means you leave its conversion tuning, its fraud signals, its accessibility work and its native payment surfaces. You take on form maintenance as a theme dependency. Your analytics change shape, because the orders arrive through a different path. If you have not worked through that trade-off, do it before comparing these two at all — our piece on <a href="/blog/native-checkout-vs-cod-order-form">native checkout versus a replacement COD form</a> covers it properly, and the honest conclusion there is that a lot of brands should stay in the native checkout and solve COD risk a different way.</p>

        <p>The rest of this article assumes you have already decided a form is right for you.</p>

        <h2 id="aov">EasySell's bet: the form is an AOV surface</h2>

        <p>Read EasySell's feature list and the weighting is unmistakable. Its five bullets cover a fast COD form, then "upsells, downsells, bundles, and quantity offers", then OTP and blocking rules, then partial COD deposits, then translation, multi-currency and address autocomplete. Two of the five are about order value.</p>

        <p>Its feature tags go considerably further than the bullets. The listing carries tags for cross-sell, one-click upsells, post-purchase upsells, cart upsell, checkout upsell, product page upsell, thank-you page upsell, one-click add-ons, sticky cart, cart drawer, bundles, quantity breaks, volume discounts, frequently bought together, free gifts, gift wrap, free shipping and shipping protection. The UNLIMITED plan adds an AI-powered product recommender. Its analytics tags are click-through rates, conversion rates and funnel performance — the vocabulary of a conversion tool, not a logistics one.</p>

        <p>The integrations point the same direction. The listing says it works with Checkout, Shopify Admin, Google Sheets, and then four page-builder and bundle apps: GemPages, PageFly, Kaching Bundles, and Pumper Bundles & Wide Bundles & BixGrow. That is the stack of a merchant building landing pages and offer ladders, not one tuning courier allocation.</p>

        <p>If your COD problem is mostly that your average order value is too low to absorb a shipping cost and an RTO rate, this is a coherent answer to it. Raising AOV is a legitimate RTO strategy: the same percentage of returns hurts less when each delivered order carries more margin. EasySell's listing describes more machinery for that than ours does.</p>

        <h2 id="payment">COD King's bet: the form is a payment decision</h2>

        <p>Our listing's five bullets are the COD form, OTP over SMS or WhatsApp, partial COD on risky orders, confirmation over WhatsApp, SMS or IVR with auto-cancellation of unconfirmed orders, and COD rules, COD fees and prepaid discounts. One of the five is about order value; four are about whether and on what terms the order should ship.</p>

        <p>Three specific differences fall out of that, and they are the ones worth arguing about.</p>

        <p><strong>Voice as a confirmation channel.</strong> Our listing names IVR as one of three confirmation channels. EasySell's listing describes OTP over SMS and WhatsApp and carries a "Phone confirmation" tag, but does not mention IVR or automated voice calls anywhere, and neither does tyslo.com. This matters more than it sounds in tier-3 lanes, where a WhatsApp message to a number that belongs to a shared family handset gets read by nobody and a ringing phone gets answered. It matters less if you sell into metros. Our breakdown of <a href="/blog/whatsapp-vs-sms-vs-ivr-cod-verification">WhatsApp versus SMS versus IVR</a> explains how to measure which channel actually reaches your buyers rather than guessing.</p>

        <p><strong>Rules that vary by where the order is going.</strong> Our listing describes limiting COD by pincode, cart value, product or RTO risk, plus flexible COD fees and prepaid discounts. EasySell's listing carries "Custom fees" and "Prepaid incentives" tags and an "IP blocking" tag, but does not mention pincode or ZIP-based rules. If your plan for the worst 5% of your pincodes is to switch them to prepaid-only rather than block them outright, that is a geographic rule, and one listing describes it. <a href="/blog/cod-pincode-serviceability">Pincode serviceability</a> explains why that list is usually shorter than merchants expect, and <a href="/blog/cod-risk-scoring">risk scoring</a> covers attaching actions to it.</p>

        <p><strong>The middle option on a risky order.</strong> Both describe partial COD, which is the genuinely interesting lever — ask for a small amount upfront, ship the rest on delivery, and let the buyer's willingness to pay anything at all do the filtering. On EasySell's listing, "Partial Payment" appears as an ADVANCED plan feature, which at the time of reading was the \$24.95/month tier; the bullets describe it as "partial COD deposits to reduce non-delivery risk". Ours describes it on the free tier with a percentage fee attached. Same lever, different place in the pricing. <a href="/blog/partial-cod-payment-advance-amount">How much advance to collect</a> is the part most merchants get wrong regardless of which app they use.</p>

        <p>One thing to ask both vendors rather than infer: neither listing mentions writing Shopify order tags on confirmation or cancellation. If your picking process, courier allocation rules or 3PL integration key off order tags, ask directly. This is exactly the kind of feature that exists and goes undocumented.</p>

        <h2 id="pricing">Pricing: the models are opposite, so the answer depends on your volume</h2>

        <p>This is the most consequential difference and the easiest to get wrong, because the two apps are priced on incompatible models.</p>

        <p>EasySell publishes a conventional SaaS ladder with order quotas. Forever Free is free to install and covers 60 orders per month, with upsells, downsells and quantity offers, WhatsApp and SMS messaging, basic fraud prevention, address validation, Google Sheets, multi-currency, multiple pixels and analytics. PRO is \$9.95 per month, or \$89.99 annually, for 440 orders per month, adding product bundles, form templates, quantity offers on the product page and advanced fraud prevention. ADVANCED is \$24.95 per month, or \$225.99 annually, for 10,000 orders per month, adding partial payment, a multi-language form and product-specific pixels. UNLIMITED is \$59.95 per month, or \$539.99 annually, with unlimited orders, advanced tax settings and the AI product recommender. PRO and ADVANCED charge \$0.05 for each order beyond the quota. On every plan, SMS and WhatsApp messages are charged separately.</p>

        <p>Ours publishes a structure rather than a price. The free tier is free to install, charges 1.5% on partially paid orders, and caps the COD form at 60 orders. The three paid tiers display at a nominal \$0.01 per month, which is not the real cost: the real cost is per-message rates by country plus the percentage on partially paid orders, which falls to 0.8%, then 0.5%, then nothing as you move up. The upper tiers add a local SMS gateway, branded SMS templates, bringing your own WhatsApp number at a stated per-message fee plus Meta's own charge, a Shopify Plus OTP extension at checkout, branding removal, and higher COD form caps — 500, 10,000, then unlimited. There is a seven-day free trial, and all charges bill in USD.</p>

        <p>So the comparison is structural, not numeric. EasySell's cost is predictable and rises in steps as you cross quotas; at 440 orders a month on PRO the app fee works out to roughly two cents an order, plus whatever messaging you send. Ours is variable and rises smoothly with how much you message and how many orders you part-collect on, with no app fee to speak of. Neither is cheaper in general. Flat pricing is cheaper when you verify heavily and message a lot; usage pricing is cheaper when your volume is spiky, when you verify selectively, or when you are seasonal enough that a flat fee in February pays for nothing.</p>

        <p>Two questions settle it. What is your monthly COD order count, and how stable is it across the year? And what fraction of orders do you intend to actually message or part-collect on — all of them, or only the ones a risk rule flags? If the answer to the second is "all of them", flat pricing usually wins. If it is "the worst fifth", usage pricing usually does. Either way, convert both into cost per confirmed order before deciding, because that is the only number that compares.</p>

        <h2 id="listing-details">Listing details worth checking against your own constraints</h2>

        <p>Read on 25 November 2026, EasySell's listing showed an average of 4.9 across 1,002 reviews and a launch date of 8 October 2020; ours showed 4.9 across 994 reviews and a launch date of 20 May 2021. Both figures move constantly, so treat them as a snapshot. They are also a weaker signal than they look: review volume tracks install base and how persistently a vendor asks for reviews as much as it tracks quality. Ten minutes in the one- and two-star reviews of both listings is worth more than any comparison table, this one included.</p>

        <p>Three other details may matter depending on your situation. EasySell's listing names twelve interface languages — English, Spanish, Italian, French, Arabic, German, Croatian, Portuguese in both Brazilian and European variants, Romanian, Serbian and Turkish — a set oriented towards Europe, the Middle East and Latin America. Ours names nine, including Hindi and Urdu. If you need the form itself and the admin in Hindi, check that specifically.</p>

        <p>Our listing carries Shopify's Built for Shopify badge; EasySell's listing does not display it. That badge reflects Shopify's own criteria around performance, app design and admin integration, and it is a reasonable proxy for how cleanly an app sits inside the admin — but it is a proxy, not an outcome, and plenty of excellent apps do not carry it.</p>

        <p>Finally, EasySell's listing names Google Sheets as an integration and lists address validation and Google Sheets together on its free plan. Ours carries an "Order export" tag without naming a destination. If your ops team runs on a shared sheet — and in Indian D2C a surprising number do, including some running substantial volume — that is a concrete workflow difference in EasySell's favour.</p>

        <h2 id="easysell-better">Where EasySell is the better pick</h2>

        <p>Four cases, none of them edge cases.</p>

        <p>If your real problem is average order value rather than order quality, its listing describes considerably more machinery for solving that: downsells, bundles, cross-sell, quantity breaks, volume discounts, free gifts, a recommender, and a drag-and-drop builder to arrange them. Buying a verification-first tool to solve an AOV problem is buying the wrong thing.</p>

        <p>If you need predictable costs, a flat monthly fee you can put in a budget beats a usage-based bill you have to model, and that is worth paying something for.</p>

        <p>If your stack is page builders and bundle apps, its listing names integrations with four of them by name. Integrations that already exist beat integrations you have to ask about.</p>

        <p>And if your ops run through Google Sheets, or you need the form in a European or Latin American language, its listing covers both explicitly.</p>

        <h2 id="wrong">When COD King is the wrong choice</h2>

        <p>Several cases, and we would rather you found them now than after migrating.</p>

        <p><strong>Your RTO is not an intent problem.</strong> Verification turns a doorstep refusal into a pre-dispatch cancellation, which is cheaper but is not a delivery. If your returns are driven by sizing, transit damage or orders arriving two weeks late, no verification app helps. <a href="/blog/apparel-sizing-cod-returns">Sizing</a>, <a href="/blog/packaging-damage-cod-refusals">packaging</a> and <a href="/blog/dispatch-speed-cod-orders">dispatch speed</a> are the fixes, and an app that confirms intent will cheerfully confirm intent on orders that were always coming back.</p>

        <p><strong>You are buying an upsell engine.</strong> If the main job is offer ladders, bundles and post-purchase flows, our listing describes one bullet of that and EasySell's describes most of a product.</p>

        <p><strong>Your volume is small.</strong> At a handful of COD orders a day, a person calling customers is cheaper, warmer and far more informative than any automation, and you will learn more from ten conversations than from a month of dashboards.</p>

        <p><strong>You need to stay in Shopify's native checkout.</strong> Our COD form is a replacement surface, and the checkout-level OTP extension sits on the top tier and on Shopify Plus. If leaving the native checkout is off the table and Plus is not on the cards, a good part of what makes us distinctive is out of reach — and so, for the same reason, is much of EasySell.</p>

        <p><strong>Nobody will action the output.</strong> Risk flags, COD rules and partial-payment thresholds only pay for themselves if someone changes a courier, a payment option or a pincode policy in response. If no one owns that, the app is a line item. Our wider survey, <a href="/blog/best-cod-verification-apps-shopify">how to choose a COD verification app</a>, applies the same test across the full field.</p>

        <h2 id="test">How to settle it in two weeks</h2>

        <p>Both have free entry points capped at 60 orders a month, which is enough to evaluate and not enough to run on. Install one, run it for a week on a defined slice of orders, then the other on a comparable slice. Do not run both at once on the same cohort; you will not be able to attribute anything.</p>

        <p>Record five numbers each week. Form completion rate, because a replacement form that converts worse than native checkout costs you more than RTO does. The share of orders where you actually reached the customer. The share that reached a confirmed state. Median time from order to confirmation, because that is what decides whether verification sits on your dispatch critical path. And average order value, since one of these apps is explicitly trying to move it.</p>

        <p>Then answer the question that decides it. For an order your rules flag as risky, what do you want to happen? If the answer is "cancel it", both apps describe doing that and you should choose on cost, reach and support. If the answer is "collect a small amount upfront, charge a COD fee, or make that pincode prepaid-only", then check which of those three you need and on which plan, because that is where the two diverge. And if the answer is "ship it, but attach a bundle so the margin covers the risk", that is EasySell's argument, and it is a real one.</p>

        <p>Where COD King fits, plainly: it is built for merchants whose main COD problem is deciding which orders should ship and on what terms — partial payment, COD fees, prepaid discounts and rules that vary by pincode, cart value, product or risk, with OTP and WhatsApp, SMS or IVR confirmation in front of them. If your main problem is order value rather than order quality, we are not the better tool and we would rather say so.</p>

        <h2 id="short">The short version</h2>

        <p>Both apps replace Shopify's COD checkout with a form you control, and that shared decision carries costs both vendors leave off their listings. From there, EasySell is built around raising order value — upsells, downsells, bundles, cross-sell, a recommender, page-builder and bundle integrations, Google Sheets, twelve interface languages — priced as a flat monthly ladder from free through \$9.95, \$24.95 and \$59.95 with order quotas and five-cent overage. COD King is built around the payment decision — partial COD, COD fees, prepaid discounts, COD rules by pincode, cart value, product or risk, and confirmation over WhatsApp, SMS or IVR — priced on per-message rates plus a percentage of partially paid orders that falls to zero on the top tier. Flat pricing wins if you verify everything and your volume is steady; usage pricing wins if you verify selectively or your volume is seasonal. Test each for a week, measure cost per confirmed order alongside form completion rate, and read the low-star reviews on both listings before you read either vendor's claims, ours included.</p>
