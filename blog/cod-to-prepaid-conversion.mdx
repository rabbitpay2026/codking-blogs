      <div className="ck-summary"><div className="ck-summary-label">Summary</div><p>Prepaid orders are worth more than COD orders because they almost never come back. This is how to move your mix toward prepaid using price, timing, and trust — rather than by blocking COD and hoping customers comply.</p></div>

      <div className="ck-body">
        <p className="ck-lede">A prepaid order and a COD order of the same value are not worth the same to your business. The prepaid order is paid for, nearly always delivers, and carries no collection cost. The COD order might come back, and if it does you pay twice for shipping and earn nothing. Shifting even a modest share of your mix from the second to the first is usually the highest-margin change available to a D2C brand in India.</p>

        <p>The difficulty is that COD is why many of your customers trust you enough to order at all. Cash on Delivery accounts for roughly 60 to 65 percent of Indian online orders by Cashfree's estimate, and for a first-time buyer on an unfamiliar brand it is a reasonable hedge. Remove it and you do not convert those customers to prepaid — you lose them.</p>

        <p>So this is not a blocking exercise. It is a pricing and trust exercise.</p>

        <h2 id="economics">Start by knowing what COD actually costs you</h2>

        <p>Most brands cannot answer this, which is why their COD fee is a guess. Work out the real figure for your business:</p>

        <p>Take your COD RTO rate. For each returned parcel you pay forward shipping, return shipping, and packaging, and you carry the stock cost for the round trip. Add the collection charge your courier or aggregator levies on COD. Add the operational time spent on verification and NDR handling. Divide the total by all COD orders, not just the failed ones.</p>

        <p>That per-order number is the honest cost of offering COD. For most Indian D2C brands it lands somewhere well above the COD fee they are currently charging, which means every COD order is quietly subsidised by prepaid ones. Once you know the figure, every decision below becomes arithmetic rather than instinct.</p>

        <h2 id="price-gap">Price the gap, do not hide the option</h2>

        <p>The mechanism that moves the mix is a visible price difference between paying now and paying later. You can build it two ways, and the choice matters more than the amount.</p>

        <p><strong>A COD fee</strong> adds a charge to Cash on Delivery orders. It is honest — the order does cost more — and it recovers some of the cost from the orders that create it. It also reads as a penalty, and some customers abandon rather than switch.</p>

        <p><strong>A prepaid discount</strong> takes the same gap and frames it as a reward for paying upfront. Identical economics, considerably better reception. Customers who were going to choose COD see a reason to reconsider rather than a fee to resent.</p>

        <p>The gap needs to be large enough to notice and small enough not to look punitive. A gap so small it reads as a rounding error moves nobody; one that looks like a surcharge for being poor damages the brand. Free shipping on prepaid, with standard shipping charged on COD, is a clean version of this that sidesteps the fee framing entirely.</p>

        <p>Test the size of the gap before settling on it. The metric to watch is not prepaid share alone — it is prepaid share together with total orders. A gap that lifts prepaid share while dropping total orders has not worked.</p>

        <h2 id="timing">The three moments you can ask</h2>

        <p>Where you make the prepaid case changes how well it lands.</p>

        <p><strong>At checkout.</strong> The customer is deciding. This is where the price gap does its work, and where most of your conversion will come from. Show both options with the difference stated plainly, rather than burying COD behind a toggle.</p>

        <p><strong>Immediately after the order.</strong> The order is placed as COD. Within minutes, a message offers a discount to switch to prepaid, with a payment link. This converts well because intent is still high and the customer is already committed to the purchase — you are only changing how they pay. The window is short; a message that arrives the next morning performs far worse than one that arrives in ten minutes.</p>

        <p><strong>At the verification step.</strong> You are already contacting the customer to confirm the order. That same message can carry a prepaid option. The confirmation is a natural reason to make contact, so the prepaid ask arrives as part of a helpful message rather than as a sales push. This is the most underused of the three.</p>

        <h2 id="partial">Partial payment as the middle path</h2>

        <p>Some customers will not pay in full upfront but will pay something. A partial advance — a portion of the order value collected at checkout, the balance on delivery — captures those customers entirely.</p>

        <p>It works on two levels. It filters intent, because a customer who pays anything upfront has committed. And it caps your downside, because the advance covers some or all of your shipping exposure if the order still comes back. Done well it converts a slice of orders that a prepaid-only option would have lost outright. The question of how much to ask for is covered in <a href="/blog/partial-cod-payment-advance-amount">How Much COD Advance to Collect</a>.</p>

        <h2 id="trust">The part that is not about price</h2>

        <p>A customer choosing COD on a first order is often not being difficult. They are managing a real risk: they do not know whether your brand will deliver, and COD is their insurance policy. Price incentives do not address that fear; trust signals do.</p>

        <p>Visible, specific reasons to pay upfront include a clear and generous return policy stated at checkout rather than buried in a footer, real reviews with substance, a reachable support channel with a human on the other end, and recognisable payment methods — UPI in particular, which carries its own trust and takes seconds.</p>

        <p>Payment friction matters as much as payment trust. If prepaid means a slow redirect, a failed gateway attempt, or a card form on a phone, customers will take COD for reasons that have nothing to do with risk. UPI intent flows remove most of that friction and are the single most effective prepaid enabler in the Indian market.</p>

        <p>And returning customers are a different population from first-time buyers. Someone who has received two orders from you has already learned you deliver. Targeting prepaid incentives at first-time buyers, where the trust gap actually is, spends less and converts more than a blanket discount that pays repeat customers to do something many would have done anyway.</p>

        <h2 id="measure">What to measure</h2>

        <p><strong>Prepaid share of orders.</strong> The headline number. Track it weekly, and separately for first-time and returning customers.</p>

        <p><strong>Total orders.</strong> The guardrail. Prepaid share rising while total orders fall means you are filtering customers out, not converting them.</p>

        <p><strong>Post-order switch rate.</strong> Of COD orders that received a prepaid offer after placement, what share switched. This tells you whether your timing and your offer are right.</p>

        <p><strong>RTO rate by payment method.</strong> Prepaid, partial, and full COD, tracked separately. This is what justifies the whole exercise, and it also tells you whether partial payment is pulling its weight.</p>

        <p><strong>Contribution margin per order by payment method.</strong> The number that settles arguments. Revenue minus product cost, shipping both ways where applicable, payment or collection fees, and RTO losses. A prepaid order at a discount often beats a full-price COD order once RTO is priced in — and if it does not, your discount is too large.</p>

        <h2 id="mistakes">Four ways this goes wrong</h2>

        <p><strong>Removing COD to force the issue.</strong> The orders do not convert, they disappear. If you want to test this, test it on one category or one price band, not across the store.</p>

        <p><strong>Making the incentive too large.</strong> A discount that exceeds the cost of the RTO risk it avoids is a loss dressed as an improvement. Bound it with the per-order COD cost you calculated at the start.</p>

        <p><strong>Treating all customers identically.</strong> First-time buyers need trust, returning buyers need convenience, high-value orders need risk management. One blanket rule serves none of them well.</p>

        <p><strong>Judging it on prepaid share alone.</strong> Prepaid share is easy to move and easy to move badly. Contribution margin per order and total order count are what tell you whether the mix shift created value or just moved it around.</p>

        <p>COD King handles the mechanics of this — COD fees, prepaid discounts, partial payments, and post-order prepaid conversion over WhatsApp — but the pricing decision is yours, and it should start with knowing what a COD order actually costs you.</p>
