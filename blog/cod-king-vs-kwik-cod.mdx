      <div className="ck-summary"><div className="ck-summary-label">Summary</div><p>These two are not the same kind of product. GoKwik's Kwik COD & Checkout replaces Shopify's checkout, adds RTO intelligence and COD blocking on top of it, and routes the transaction through a payment aggregator at a stated 3.5%. COD King sits on the checkout you already have, adds a COD form plus OTP and WhatsApp, SMS or IVR confirmation, and never touches your payment rails. So the pricing is not comparable on its face — one number includes payment processing and one does not. Everything factual below was read from each listing on 27 November 2026.</p></div>

      <div className="ck-body">
        <p className="ck-lede">The reason this comparison is usually done badly is that one side of it is quoted as \$35 a month and the other as free to install, and the conclusion writes itself. It is the wrong conclusion, because the \$35 figure comes attached to a 3.5% transaction fee that buys something COD King does not sell at all — payment processing. Once you separate the layers, the choice becomes a clean architectural question with a clear answer for most operations.</p>

        <p>A note on method. Everything factual below comes from each app's own Shopify App Store listing, read on 27 November 2026. Where a listing does not describe something, this article says exactly that rather than claiming the product cannot do it; listings are marketing pages, and shipped features routinely never reach them. One of these apps is ours, so there is a section below on where COD King is the wrong choice. Read that first if you are short on time.</p>

        <h2 id="two-listings">First, which Kwik COD you mean</h2>

        <p>GoKwik publishes two separate COD listings, and merchants conflate them constantly. Both are published by GoKwik Commerce Solutions Pvt. Ltd. c/o Fineshift Software Inc., and they are otherwise different products.</p>

        <p><strong>Kwik COD & Checkout</strong> is the substantial one. Its listing describes a checkout solution, launched 17 May 2024, with one published plan. On 27 November 2026 it displayed 4.6 across 398 reviews and listed English as its only interface language.</p>

        <p><strong>Kwik COD Form ‑ Dropshipping</strong> is a different listing: launched 3 June 2025, headline price Free, no published plans or inclusions, aimed in its own words at dropshipping, with upsell carousels, quantity discounts, auto-translated forms and country-specific address fields. It listed fourteen interface languages. On 27 November 2026 it displayed 2.8 across 2 reviews — a review count too small to carry any signal in either direction, which is worth saying plainly rather than quoting as though it meant something.</p>

        <p>The rest of this article compares COD King with <strong>Kwik COD & Checkout</strong>, because that is the product an Indian D2C brand running real COD volume would actually be evaluating. If you are a global dropshipper, the Dropshipping listing is a different conversation and its feature tags — pop-ups, custom layouts, one-click upsells, multi-language — point at it.</p>

        <h2 id="architecture">The architectural difference, which is the whole thing</h2>

        <p>Kwik COD & Checkout's five bullets open with accelerating checkout via smart login, pre-filled address and a customisable checkout. Its feature tags include a drag-and-drop editor, custom layouts, custom fields, address validation and theme colour, logo and text controls. Its single published plan includes "Customise UI/UX" and "Address Prefill". That is a product that owns the checkout surface.</p>

        <p>And its pricing line confirms how far that ownership goes: the Emerging plan is \$35 per month with "3.5% applicable on transactions by payment aggregator". A COD verification app has no reason to charge a percentage of transactions. A checkout that settles your prepaid orders does. So you are not buying an app that sits beside your payments stack; you are buying a checkout, and the payments economics come with it.</p>

        <p>COD King is the other shape. Its listing describes a one-step COD form on product pages and the cart, OTP over SMS or WhatsApp, partial COD on risky orders, confirmation over WhatsApp, SMS or IVR with auto-cancellation, and COD rules, COD fees and prepaid discounts. Its "Works with" list names Checkout, Customer accounts, Shopify Admin and a set of messaging providers — Twilio, Proweb SMS, Veevo Tech, WhatsApp. It is a layer, not a replacement for your payment rails. Whatever gateway you use keeps settling your money on whatever terms you already negotiated.</p>

        <p>That is the decision. Everything below is detail hanging off it.</p>

        <h2 id="rto-data">Where Kwik COD & Checkout is genuinely differentiated</h2>

        <p>Two things on its listing are hard for a single-store app to match, and it is worth being straight about them.</p>

        <p><strong>Network-level RTO data.</strong> Its Emerging plan lists "Access to RTO Intelligence Data" and "RTO Interventions for shoppers", and a feature bullet describes reducing RTO by "intelligently blocking COD with 70+personalized interventions". A risk model built across many merchants sees a buyer who refused three parcels at three different stores; a model built inside your own store sees a first-time customer. That asymmetry is real and it is structural — you cannot build it yourself at any price. Our own piece on <a href="/blog/cod-risk-scoring">COD risk scoring</a> is explicit that the ceiling on a single-store model is your own order history, and if your order history is thin, the ceiling is low.</p>

        <p>Two caveats to hold alongside it. First, the listing does not describe how that data is sourced, what it contains, or how a score is produced, so treat "70+ interventions" as a claim to interrogate on a call rather than a specification. Second, a shared risk signal is only useful if someone acts on it, which is the test we apply to every tool in this category.</p>

        <p><strong>Checkout conversion, not just COD risk.</strong> Smart login, address prefill and a checkout you can restyle act on every order, prepaid included. COD King acts on the COD slice. If your prepaid conversion rate is the bigger number on your P\&L, a checkout product has more surface area to move than a verification layer does. Our <a href="/blog/shopify-checkout-optimization">checkout optimisation</a> notes cover which of those levers actually move anything.</p>

        <p>Its listing also claims "35+ plug & play integrations" in a feature bullet while its "Works with" field names only Checkout. Those two statements are not contradictory — the Works with field is a Shopify-managed list and vendors populate it inconsistently — but it means you should ask for the integration list in writing if a specific 3PL, WMS or marketing tool is load-bearing for you. Same advice we would give about our own listing.</p>

        <h2 id="codking-side">Where COD King's listing describes things Kwik's does not</h2>

        <p>Three differences worth arguing about, stated as listing facts rather than product claims.</p>

        <p><strong>Voice as a confirmation channel.</strong> COD King's listing names IVR as one of three confirmation channels. Kwik COD & Checkout's listing carries "Phone confirmation" and "SMS confirmation" tags and a "One-time password (OTP)" tag, but does not mention IVR or automated voice calls. In tier-3 lanes this is not a footnote: a WhatsApp message to a shared family handset gets read by nobody, and a ringing phone gets answered. In metros it matters much less. Our breakdown of <a href="/blog/whatsapp-vs-sms-vs-ivr-cod-verification">WhatsApp versus SMS versus IVR</a> explains how to measure reach per channel on your own order book instead of arguing about it.</p>

        <p><strong>Interface language.</strong> Kwik COD & Checkout's listing names English as its only language. COD King's names nine, including Hindi and Urdu. If your warehouse or support team works in Hindi, or you want the buyer-facing form in Hindi, check that specifically with both vendors — buyer-facing text is often configurable even when the admin is not, and a language list on a listing does not settle it either way.</p>

        <p><strong>A free entry point.</strong> COD King's free tier is free to install, charges 1.5% on partially paid orders, caps the COD form at 60 orders, and includes OTP verification, confirmation and tracking messages, abandoned cart recovery and chat support. Kwik COD & Checkout publishes one plan at \$35 per month with no free tier shown on the listing and no trial label. That changes how you evaluate: one you can switch on this afternoon on a slice of orders, the other is a procurement decision.</p>

        <p>One thing to ask both vendors rather than infer: neither listing mentions writing Shopify order tags on confirmation or cancellation. If your picking rules, courier allocation or 3PL integration key off order tags — and <a href="/blog/courier-allocation-cod-orders">courier allocation</a> usually should — ask directly. This is exactly the sort of feature that exists and goes undocumented.</p>

        <h2 id="pricing">Pricing: separate the layers before you compare them</h2>

        <p>Kwik COD & Checkout publishes one plan. Emerging is \$35 per month, with "3.5% applicable on transactions by payment aggregator", and includes COD management, access to RTO Intelligence Data, RTO interventions for shoppers, COD blocking for unlimited pincodes, discount and gift card application, UI/UX customisation, order notifications and address prefill. Charges bill in USD every 30 days, and the listing points to gokwik.co for further pricing options.</p>

        <p>COD King publishes a structure rather than a price. The free tier is free to install at 1.5% on partially paid orders with the COD form capped at 60 orders. The three paid tiers display at a nominal \$0.01 per month, which is not the real cost: the real cost is per-message rates by country plus a percentage on partially paid orders, which falls to 0.8%, then 0.5%, then nothing as you move up. The upper tiers add COD rules and flexible COD fees, discounted messaging, a local SMS gateway, branded SMS templates, bringing your own WhatsApp number at a stated \$0.001 per message plus Meta's own charge, a Shopify Plus OTP extension at checkout, branding removal, and COD form caps of 500, 10,000, then unlimited. There is a seven-day free trial and charges bill in USD.</p>

        <p>To compare these honestly you have to split the 3.5% out. That line is payment processing, and you are paying someone for payment processing today. So the comparison is: what does your current gateway charge on a prepaid order, and does GoKwik's rate plus \$35 beat it once you also delete whatever you spend on checkout and COD tooling? If your current blended gateway rate is at or below 3.5%, the \$35 is the whole incremental app cost and the comparison is straightforward. If you are paying meaningfully more than that, the aggregator rate may pay for the app on its own, and this stops being a tooling decision and becomes a payments one — which means it needs your finance lead in the room, not just ops.</p>

        <p>Against that, COD King's cost is variable and scales with how much you message and how many orders you part-collect on. There is no flat fee to speak of and no transaction percentage on prepaid or COD orders. That is cheaper when you verify selectively — say, the worst fifth of orders by risk — and when your volume is seasonal enough that a flat monthly fee in February pays for nothing. It is more expensive when you message every order every time. Convert both into cost per confirmed order before deciding; it is the only number that compares.</p>

        <h2 id="listing-details">Listing details checked today</h2>

        <p>On 27 November 2026, Kwik COD & Checkout displayed 4.6 across 398 reviews, launched 17 May 2024, with 363 five-star, 11 four-star, 2 three-star, no two-star and 22 one-star reviews. COD King displayed 4.9 across 994 reviews, launched 20 May 2021, with 980 five-star, 1 four-star, 1 three-star, 3 two-star and 9 one-star. Both move constantly, and both are weaker signals than they look: review volume tracks install base and how persistently a vendor asks for reviews at least as much as it tracks quality, and a product launched in 2024 has had less time to accumulate either.</p>

        <p>What is worth your time is reading the one- and two-star reviews on both listings. Checkout replacements fail in a specific and recognisable way — theme conflicts, a broken flow on one device class, a settlement question — and those reviews will tell you which failure modes are live right now far better than any comparison table, this one included.</p>

        <p>COD King's listing carries Shopify's Built for Shopify badge; neither Kwik COD listing displayed it on 27 November 2026. That badge reflects Shopify's own criteria for performance, app design and admin integration, and it is a reasonable proxy for how cleanly an app sits inside the admin. It is a proxy, not an outcome, and plenty of good apps do not carry it — including, structurally, products that replace the checkout rather than extend it.</p>

        <h2 id="kwik-better">Where Kwik COD & Checkout is the better pick</h2>

        <p>Four cases, none of them edge cases.</p>

        <p>If your store is young or low-volume and your own order history is too thin to build a usable risk model, network RTO data beats anything you can derive in-house. That is the strongest argument on its listing and you should weight it heavily.</p>

        <p>If you are already reconsidering your payments stack, a product that bundles checkout, COD controls and processing into one contract and one \$35 line plus 3.5% may genuinely be cheaper all-in than a gateway plus a checkout app plus a verification app. Model it properly before dismissing it.</p>

        <p>If your bigger problem is prepaid conversion rather than COD quality, a checkout product acts on every order and a verification layer acts on a slice.</p>

        <p>And if you want one vendor and one support channel for checkout, COD and payments rather than three, that is a legitimate operational preference and it costs real money to ignore.</p>

        <h2 id="wrong">When COD King is the wrong choice</h2>

        <p>Several cases, and we would rather you found them now than after migrating.</p>

        <p><strong>Your RTO is not an intent problem.</strong> Verification converts a doorstep refusal into a pre-dispatch cancellation, which is cheaper but is not a delivery. If your returns are driven by sizing, transit damage or parcels arriving two weeks late, no verification app helps. <a href="/blog/apparel-sizing-cod-returns">Sizing</a>, <a href="/blog/packaging-damage-cod-refusals">packaging</a> and <a href="/blog/dispatch-speed-cod-orders">dispatch speed</a> are the fixes, and an app that confirms intent will cheerfully confirm intent on orders that were always coming back.</p>

        <p><strong>You need shared, cross-merchant risk data.</strong> We score on your own order history, your own pincode performance and your own refusal patterns. If your store is too new to have any of that, we are the wrong tool for the first few months and a network product is the right one.</p>

        <p><strong>You are buying a checkout.</strong> If the job is restyling checkout, accelerating login and prefilling addresses across every order, that is not what we do. Our COD form is a capture surface for COD orders, and our checkout-level OTP extension sits on the top tier and on Shopify Plus.</p>

        <p><strong>Your volume is small.</strong> At a handful of COD orders a day, a person calling customers is cheaper, warmer and far more informative than any automation, and you will learn more from ten conversations than from a month of dashboards.</p>

        <p><strong>Nobody will action the output.</strong> Risk flags, COD rules and partial-payment thresholds only pay for themselves if someone changes a courier, a payment option or a pincode policy in response. If no one owns that, the app is a line item. Our wider survey, <a href="/blog/best-cod-verification-apps-shopify">how to choose a COD verification app</a>, applies the same test across the field.</p>

        <h2 id="decide">How to settle it</h2>

        <p>These two cannot be A/B tested against each other the way two verification apps can, because one of them changes your checkout and your settlement. Running both at once on the same cohort will tell you nothing. So the sequence is different.</p>

        <p>Start with the architectural question, because it is dispositive. Are you willing to replace Shopify's checkout and move your payment processing? If the answer is no — and for a lot of brands with a working gateway, a negotiated rate and a theme they have spent money on, it is no — then Kwik COD & Checkout is out of scope and you are choosing among verification layers, which is a different comparison and one we cover in <a href="/blog/best-cod-verification-apps-shopify">our survey of the field</a>. If the answer is yes or maybe, this becomes a payments evaluation, and the right next step is pulling your last twelve months of gateway statements and your blended processing rate.</p>

        <p>If you are staying on your current checkout, do the cheap test. Install a verification layer on the free tier, run it for two weeks on a defined slice — say every COD order above a value threshold, or every order into your worst twenty pincodes — and record five numbers each week: form completion rate, the share of orders where you actually reached the customer, the share that reached a confirmed state, median time from order to confirmation, and your RTO rate on the tested slice against an untested control. If verification does not move the last number, nothing downstream of it matters.</p>

        <p>And read <a href="/blog/native-checkout-vs-cod-order-form">native checkout versus a replacement COD form</a> before either, because the honest conclusion there is that a lot of brands should stay in the native checkout and solve COD risk a different way entirely.</p>

        <p>Where COD King fits, plainly: it is built for merchants who want to keep the checkout and the gateway they have, and decide which COD orders should ship and on what terms — partial payment, COD fees, prepaid discounts and rules that vary by pincode, cart value, product or risk, with OTP and WhatsApp, SMS or IVR confirmation in front of them. If you want a new checkout with payments attached and network RTO data behind it, that is a different product and we are not it.</p>

        <h2 id="short">The short version</h2>

        <p>Kwik COD & Checkout replaces your checkout, adds COD blocking and network RTO intelligence, and routes transactions through a payment aggregator at \$35 a month plus 3.5%; its listing names English only, launched in May 2024, and displayed 4.6 across 398 reviews on 27 November 2026. COD King sits on the checkout you have, adds a COD form, OTP and WhatsApp, SMS or IVR confirmation, partial COD, COD fees and pincode-level rules, and prices on messaging plus a percentage of partially paid orders that reaches zero on the top tier; it displayed 4.9 across 994 reviews and carries the Built for Shopify badge. The \$35 is not the comparison — the 3.5% is, because it includes payment processing you already pay for elsewhere. If you are open to changing checkout and payments and your store is too new for its own risk data, the network product has the stronger case. If your gateway and theme are settled and the problem is deciding which COD orders should ship, the layer is the cheaper and less invasive answer. Read the low-star reviews on both listings before you read either vendor's claims, ours included.</p>
